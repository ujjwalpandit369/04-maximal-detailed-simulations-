Interactive Simulation: Lattice Boltzmann Fluid Dynamics

Simulating Complex Flows with Mesoscopic Particle Models

How to Simulate a Fluid

How would you simulate a fluid like air or water? You could try to track every single molecule, but that's computationally impossible. Or, you could solve the complex, continuous Navier-Stokes equations, which is mathematically very difficult, especially with complex boundaries. The **Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM)** offers a third way, which is both elegant and powerful.

Instead of thinking about the macroscopic fluid or the microscopic molecules, LBM operates at a middle ("mesoscopic") scale. It models the fluid as collections of fictitious particles on a grid. At each grid point, we only keep track of how many particles are moving in a few discrete directions (e.g., up, down, left, right, and diagonals). The simulation then proceeds with two simple, repeated steps:

  1. Stream: All particle populations move to the next grid point in the direction they are headed.
  2. Collide: At each grid point, the incoming particle populations interact and redistribute themselves to get closer to a local fluid equilibrium.

Amazingly, the collective result of these two simple, local steps perfectly reproduces the behavior of a real fluid. It's a beautiful example of how macroscopic phenomena emerge from simple underlying rules.

Beginner’s Guide to the Simulation

  1. The Kármán Vortex Street: When the simulation starts, you'll see a fluid flowing from left to right around a cylinder. Notice the beautiful, swirling pattern of vortices that detach from the cylinder in an alternating pattern. This is a famous phenomenon called the **Kármán vortex street**, seen everywhere from rivers flowing past bridge pylons to cloud patterns in the atmosphere. The colors represent fluid speed (brighter = faster).
  2. Change the Viscosity: Use the **Viscosity** slider. This controls how "syrupy" the fluid is.
    • At high viscosity, the flow is smooth and orderly (laminar). The vortices may disappear entirely.
    • At low viscosity, the flow is more chaotic and the vortex street is more pronounced.
  3. Draw Your Own Obstacles: Click and drag your mouse on the simulation canvas to draw your own obstacles and see how the fluid flows around them! Use the "Clear Obstacles" button to start over.

Core Theory: The D2Q9 LBM Model

This simulation uses the **D2Q9** model, which stands for "2 Dimensions, 9 Velocities". It's a standard LBM model for 2D flows. The state of each cell in our grid is not a single velocity vector, but a set of 9 numbers, fi, representing the density of a particle population moving along one of the 9 discrete velocity vectors ci.

The **Lattice Boltzmann Equation** describes the evolution of these populations:

fi(x + ciΔt, t + Δt) - fi(x, t) = Ωi(f)

This equation is elegantly split into two steps:

1. Streaming

The left side of the equation is the streaming step. Each population fi is simply moved from its current grid cell x to the neighboring cell x + ciΔt.

f'i(x, t) = fi(x - ciΔt, t)

2. Collision

The right side, Ωi, is the collision operator. It describes how particle populations are redistributed at a single grid cell. We use the simple Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) operator:

Ωi = -(1/τ) * (fi - fieq)

Here, τ is the relaxation time, which determines the fluid's kinematic viscosity via ν = cs²(τ - 0.5)Δt. fieq is the **equilibrium distribution function**. This is the distribution that the particle populations would have if the fluid at that point were in perfect equilibrium. It depends only on the macroscopic fluid density ρ and velocity u, which are calculated from the pre-collision populations:

ρ = Σi fi and ρu = Σi fici

The genius of LBM is that even though the underlying model is simple particles on a grid, by carefully choosing the equilibrium function, one can prove that the macroscopic behavior of ρ and u rigorously follows the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations of real fluid dynamics.

Boundary Conditions

One of the great strengths of LBM is handling complex boundaries. For the solid obstacles in this simulation, a simple **bounce-back** rule is used. When a particle population streaming from a fluid cell would enter a solid cell, it is simply reflected back to where it came from, in the opposite direction. This simple rule effectively enforces the no-slip (zero velocity) boundary condition.
