Interactive Simulation: Cloud Microphysics and Droplet Collision–Coalescence

This simulation explores a fundamental process in atmospheric science: how tiny cloud droplets grow into raindrops large enough to fall to the ground. While the initial formation of droplets occurs through condensation, this process is too slow to create rain on its own. The primary mechanism for rain formation in "warm clouds" (those without ice) is the collision and coalescence of droplets, a process driven by gravity and fluid dynamics.

The Droplet Growth Bottleneck

Clouds form when water vapor in the atmosphere cools and condenses onto tiny aerosol particles called Cloud Condensation Nuclei (CCN). This initial process quickly creates a large number of very small droplets, typically around 10-20 micrometers in radius. However, at this point, a "bottleneck" occurs. All these droplets are of a similar size and fall at roughly the same, very slow terminal velocity. Furthermore, growth by condensation slows down dramatically as the droplets get larger. For these droplets to grow into raindrops (which are about 100 times larger in radius and 1 million times larger in volume), a different mechanism is needed.

Growth by Collision and Coalescence

The solution to the bottleneck is collision-coalescence. Due to slight variations, some droplets become slightly larger than others. A larger droplet has a higher terminal velocity, so it falls faster than the smaller droplets below it. As it falls, it can collide with and merge (or coalesce) with the smaller, slower-moving droplets in its path. Each collision adds to the larger droplet's mass, making it even bigger and causing it to fall even faster, leading to more collisions. This runaway process allows a few "lucky" droplets to grow very rapidly, sweeping up many smaller droplets and eventually becoming large and heavy enough to fall as rain.

The efficiency of this process is described by the collision kernel, which depends on the sizes of the two colliding droplets. Very small droplets are often swept aside by the airflow around a larger falling droplet and avoid collision. Therefore, there is an optimal size range for efficient collection. This simulation visualizes this complex, stochastic process and the resulting evolution of the droplet size distribution.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. What are Cloud Condensation Nuclei (CCN)?

CCN are tiny aerosol particles in the atmosphere (like dust, salt from sea spray, or sulfates) upon which water vapor can condense. Pure water vapor requires very high supersaturation to form droplets on its own, but these aerosol particles provide a surface that makes condensation much easier, allowing cloud droplets to form at the humidity levels typically found in the atmosphere.

2. What is "terminal velocity"?

Terminal velocity is the constant speed that a freely falling object eventually reaches when the resistance of the medium (in this case, air drag) through which it is falling equals the force of gravity. A larger, heavier droplet has a higher terminal velocity than a smaller, lighter one.

3. Why is growth by condensation so slow for larger droplets?

As a droplet grows, the rate of condensation slows down because it becomes harder for water vapor molecules to diffuse to the droplet's surface through the surrounding air. Furthermore, as the droplet grows, it also needs to dissipate the latent heat released by condensation, which also slows the process. This leads to the "condensation bottleneck."

4. What is the difference between "collision" and "coalescence"?

Collision is just the event of two droplets touching. Coalescence is when two colliding droplets successfully merge into a single, larger droplet. Not all collisions lead to coalescence; sometimes the droplets can bounce off each other, especially if they are very small.

5. What factors determine the collision efficiency?

The collision efficiency depends on the sizes of both the larger "collector" drop and the smaller droplet. If the small droplet is too small, it will be carried around the collector drop by the airflow (hydrodynamic effect) and will not collide. The efficiency is highest for a collector drop of a certain size and smaller droplets that are large enough to not get swept aside.

6. How does the primary simulation work?

It's a simplified model of a droplet population. Each droplet is given a size and a corresponding terminal velocity. In each time step, the droplets move downwards. The simulation checks for overlaps (collisions). When a collision occurs, the two droplets are removed and replaced by a single new droplet whose volume is the sum of the original two, placed at their center of mass.

7. What is the "droplet size distribution"?

It is a histogram or plot that shows how many droplets exist in different size categories. Initially, cloud droplet distributions are very narrow, with most droplets having a similar size. The collision-coalescence process acts to broaden this distribution, creating a "tail" of very large droplets, which eventually become rain.

8. What is the "collision kernel"?

The collision kernel, K(R, r), is a mathematical function that gives the volume of air a large collector droplet of radius R sweeps out per unit time with respect to smaller droplets of radius r. It is proportional to the cross-sectional area of the collector drop, the difference in their terminal velocities, and the collision efficiency.

9. What is "warm rain"?

Warm rain is precipitation that forms in clouds that are entirely at temperatures above freezing (0°C). In these clouds, the collision-coalescence process is the only mechanism that can form raindrops. This is common in the tropics.

10. How is "cold rain" different?

In colder clouds, which contain a mixture of supercooled water droplets and ice crystals, a different process called the Bergeron-Findeisen process dominates. Water vapor has a lower saturation vapor pressure over ice than over water, so water vapor will preferentially deposit onto the ice crystals, causing them to grow very rapidly at the expense of the evaporating water droplets. These large ice crystals then fall as snow or melt into rain.

11. How does turbulence affect this process?

Turbulence has complex effects. It can increase the rate of collisions by mixing the droplets and creating localized regions of high droplet concentration. However, very strong turbulence can also prevent collisions by separating droplets. This is an active area of research.

12. Why do the droplets in the simulation have different colors?

The color is used to represent the droplet's size or radius. For example, small droplets might be blue, medium ones green, and the largest, rain-sized drops shown in red, to make the growth process easier to see.

13. How long does it take to form a raindrop?

Starting from a typical cloud droplet of 20 micrometers, it can take about 20 minutes to an hour for the collision-coalescence process to produce a raindrop of about 2 millimeters. The exact time depends heavily on the liquid water content and droplet concentration in the cloud.

14. What happens when a raindrop gets too big?

As a raindrop falls, air drag flattens its bottom and creates a depression. If it gets too large (typically over 5 mm in diameter), the air pressure from below becomes strong enough to overcome the surface tension holding it together, and the drop breaks up into several smaller droplets.

15. How is the terminal velocity calculated?

For small droplets, terminal velocity is proportional to the radius squared (v ∝ r²), a result from balancing gravity with Stokes drag. For larger drops, the relationship is more complex as turbulence and non-spherical shapes become important.

16. What does the "Initial Mean Radius" slider do?

It controls the average radius of the initial population of droplets when the simulation is reset. A larger initial mean radius will generally lead to a faster onset of the collision-coalescence process, as there are more droplets that are large enough to be efficient collectors.

17. What is "autoconversion"?

In large-scale weather and climate models, it's impossible to simulate every single droplet. Instead, they use "parameterizations." Autoconversion is a term in these models that represents the rate at which cloud water (small droplets) is converted into rain water (large droplets) via the collision-coalescence process.

18. Can electric fields affect coalescence?

Yes. Clouds, especially thunderstorms, have strong electric fields. These fields can induce charges on the droplets, and the electrostatic forces can significantly enhance the collision efficiency, particularly for very small droplets that would otherwise be swept aside.

19. Why is this process called "stochastic"?

It's called stochastic (random) because whether any two given droplets collide is a matter of chance. The growth of any single droplet is not deterministic. However, the evolution of the *entire population* can be described statistically by the "stochastic collection equation."

20. How do scientists study this in the real world?

They use research aircraft equipped with sophisticated probes that can fly through clouds and measure the droplet size distribution directly. They also use ground-based radar and laboratory experiments to study the details of droplet interactions.

21. Does this process happen in all clouds?

No. For collision-coalescence to be efficient, the cloud needs to have a high liquid water content, a broad initial droplet size distribution, and be sufficiently deep to allow time for the process to occur. Thin, wispy clouds may never produce rain.
