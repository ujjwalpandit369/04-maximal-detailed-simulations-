Overview

This simulation visualizes the dynamics of a cosmic texture, one of the most exotic and complex types of topological defects. Unlike localized defects like monopoles or strings, a texture is a delocalized, twisted configuration of a field that extends over a large region of spacetime. They are unstable and seek to unwind themselves. This simulation initializes a "skyrmion" texture, a configuration where a vector field smoothly wraps around a sphere. Driven by the need to minimize its gradient energy, this twisted field configuration will spontaneously contract and collapse. You will witness the initially widespread texture shrink, concentrating its energy into a smaller and smaller region, before finally unwinding completely in a violent central event. The collapse of a texture could be a powerful source of gravitational waves or leave a detectable imprint on the Cosmic Microwave Background, making their study a fascinating, if speculative, area of cosmology.

Full Exposition

Beginner's Guide: Untangling a Cosmic Knot

Imagine you have a large, flexible sheet of rubber, and at every point on the sheet, there's a tiny arrow that can point in any 3D direction. Now, imagine you twist this sheet in a very particular way. At the center, all arrows point straight up. Far away from the center, all arrows point straight down. In between, the arrows smoothly transition from up to down, pointing out sideways and at various angles. You've created a "knot" or a "twist" in the field of arrows. This configuration is a texture. It doesn't have a single point or line of defect; the "defect" is the entire twisted arrangement itself.

This knot is not stable. The sheet wants to relax and flatten out to its lowest energy state, where all the arrows point in the same direction. To do this, the twisted region will start to shrink. The area where the arrows are pointing in complex directions will get smaller and smaller, contracting towards the center. Finally, the entire knot will collapse at a single point, releasing all the energy that was stored in the twist. This is a texture collapse. This simulation shows a 2D version of this, where the color represents the direction of the arrow. You can watch the complex, colorful pattern shrink and simplify until it vanishes, leaving behind just a single color (a uniform vacuum state).

Core Theory: Homotopy and Non-Local Defects

Textures are associated with the breaking of large, non-Abelian global symmetry groups. Specifically, they occur when the third homotopy group of the vacuum manifold `M` is non-trivial, i.e., `π₃(M) ≠ I`. For a theory with `SU(2)` symmetry breaking to nothing, the vacuum manifold is `S³` (a 3-sphere). For `SO(3)` breaking to `SO(2)`, the manifold is `S²` (a 2-sphere). It is the latter case that gives rise to the classic texture, as `π₃(S²) = Z`. This means that there are distinct, topologically stable ways to map the 3-sphere (representing spacetime at infinity) to the `S²` vacuum manifold.

A texture configuration is a "knot" in the field. A classic example is the "skyrmion" configuration of a three-component vector field `n(x)` of unit length. The energy of the texture is stored in the gradients of the field:
E = ∫ d³x (1/2) (∇n)²
Unlike other defects, there is no potential energy term forcing the field out of the vacuum. The field is in the vacuum manifold everywhere. The energy comes purely from the field's spatial variation. Because of this, textures are unstable to collapse. A texture of size R can lower its energy by shrinking. A simple scaling argument shows that if you scale the configuration by a factor `λ`, `x -> λx`, the energy scales as `E -> E/λ`. The energy can be arbitrarily reduced by shrinking the texture (`λ -> 0`).

This leads to the collapse. The texture will contract, in principle to a point, at speeds approaching the speed of light. As it collapses, the gradients become steeper and steeper, and the energy becomes highly concentrated. Eventually, the microscopic physics of the field (which our simple gradient-energy model neglects) will become important, and the energy will be released as a burst of scalar particles or other radiation. It has been speculated that the collapse of a sufficiently large texture could be responsible for the mysterious CMB Cold Spot, an unusually large and cold region in the map of the cosmic microwave background.
