Interactive Simulation: Cellular Automaton Models

Exploring Emergent Complexity from Simple Rules

What is a Cellular Automaton?

A **Cellular Automaton (CA)** is a surprisingly simple yet powerful concept. Imagine a grid of "cells," like a checkerboard. Each cell can be in one of a few possible states (e.g., on/off, black/white, spin up/spin down). The entire system evolves in discrete time steps. At each step, the new state of a cell is determined by a simple set of rules based on the current state of its neighbors.

From these local, simple rules, incredibly complex and unexpected global patterns can emerge. CAs are used to model a vast range of physical and biological systems, from fluid dynamics and crystal growth to the patterns on a seashell and the flocking of birds. They are a perfect example of **emergence**: the idea that complex systems and patterns can arise out of a multiplicity of simple interactions.

Model 1: Conway's Game of Life

This is the most famous cellular automaton. It's not a "game" in the traditional sense, but a system that evolves on its own. Each cell is either "alive" or "dead."

  • Rules for a new generation:
  • A dead cell with exactly three live neighbors becomes a live cell (reproduction).
  • A live cell with two or three live neighbors stays alive (survival).
  • In all other cases, a cell dies or remains dead (overpopulation or loneliness).

Despite these simple rules, Life can produce an astonishing variety of patterns, including stable "still lifes," oscillating "oscillators," and moving "spaceships" like the famous glider. It has even been proven to be Turing complete, meaning it can theoretically compute anything a universal computer can.

Model 2: Forest Fire Model

This CA models the spread of a fire through a forest and demonstrates a concept called **self-organized criticality**. The system naturally evolves into a "critical" state where a small event (a single lightning strike) can trigger a chain reaction of any size.

  • States: Empty, Tree, or Burning.
  • Rules:
    1. A burning tree turns into an empty space.
    2. A tree with at least one burning neighbor catches fire.
    3. An empty space grows a tree with a small probability p_growth.
    4. A tree can spontaneously catch fire (lightning) with a very small probability p_fire.

Watch how the forest density fluctuates and how fires clear patches, creating a dynamic equilibrium. This type of model is used to understand phenomena like earthquakes, avalanches, and market crashes.

Model 3: The Ising Model

The Ising Model is a cornerstone of statistical mechanics, originally created to model ferromagnetism. Each cell is a "spin" that can be either up (+1) or down (-1). Spins prefer to align with their neighbors.

  • Rules (Metropolis Algorithm):
    1. Pick a random spin and calculate the energy change (ΔE) if it were flipped. The energy is lower if spins are aligned.
    2. If flipping lowers the energy (ΔE < 0), always do it.
    3. If flipping increases the energy (ΔE > 0), only do it with a probability of exp(-ΔE / T), where T is the temperature.

At high temperatures, thermal energy dominates, and the spins are randomly oriented (a paramagnet). As you lower the temperature below a critical point, the spins' desire to align wins out, and large domains of aligned spins form, creating a net magnetization (a ferromagnet). This is a classic **phase transition**.
