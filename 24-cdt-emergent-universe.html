Beginner's Guide: Finding the Shape in the Static

The previous simulation showed the microscopic view of a Causal Dynamical Triangulations (CDT) universe: a chaotic, fluctuating "quantum foam" of spacetime triangles. It's hard to see any recognizable universe in that microscopic mess. So how does the stable, large-scale universe we know emerge from that? By measuring and averaging.

This simulation does exactly that. On the left, you see the same fluctuating quantum geometry as before. But on the right, we are performing a measurement. At every step of the simulation, we measure the "size" or "volume" of the universe at each slice of time. We then average this measurement over thousands of Monte Carlo steps. Initially, the plot on the right is noisy and chaotic, just like the geometry on the left. But as the simulation runs and more and more measurements are averaged, a clear, stable shape begins to emerge from the noise. This is the emergent universe.

The remarkable discovery of CDT is that the shape that emerges is not random. For a wide range of starting parameters, the average shape of the quantum spacetime turns out to be a well-behaved, "de Sitter" universe—a universe with a positive cosmological constant, which is believed to be a good description of our own universe during the very early period of cosmic inflation, and possibly its ultimate future. You are watching, in real-time, how a stable, classical spacetime with a definite shape can arise from the collective sum of all possible quantum fluctuations.

Core Theory: Phase Structure and the Emergence of de Sitter Space

A key success of the Causal Dynamical Triangulations (CDT) program is the discovery of a rich phase structure and, in particular, a phase that resembles a physically realistic de Sitter universe. The simulation on the left is sampling configurations from the path integral; the plot on the right is measuring an observable quantity from that sample.

The primary observable is the volume profile, `N(t)`, which is the number of simplices (or vertices, which is proportional) at a given time slice `t`. A single configuration `T` will have a very jagged, random-looking `N(t)`. However, the expectation value of this observable, averaged over the entire path integral, ``, is a well-defined quantity that describes the macroscopic shape of the emergent spacetime.

This simulation calculates this running average. The Monte Carlo algorithm first runs for a number of "thermalization" steps to ensure it has forgotten its simple initial configuration and is sampling representative geometries from the true distribution. Then, it begins measuring `N(t)` at each step and adds it to a running average. The law of large numbers ensures that as the simulation proceeds, this running average converges to the true expectation value ``.

In 2+1 dimensions (and also in the 1+1D toy model shown here), the CDT phase diagram, as a function of the action parameters like `k₀`, reveals several phases:

  • A "crumpled" phase where the universe has a very high, possibly infinite, Hausdorff dimension and is not like a smooth manifold.
  • A "branched polymer" or "sequential" phase where the universe has a low dimension and does not exhibit extended structure.
  • A crucial third phase, dubbed the "de Sitter" phase or "Phase C." In this phase, the measured volume profile `` is found to match, with high precision, the volume profile of a classical de Sitter spacetime (the maximally symmetric solution to Einstein's equations with a positive cosmological constant).
The fact that a stable, extended de Sitter universe emerges from this non-perturbative, background-independent model of quantum gravity is considered a major achievement of the CDT program and a strong hint that it may be on the right track.
