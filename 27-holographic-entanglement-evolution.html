Beginner's Guide: The Geometry of Entanglement

Quantum entanglement is one of the strangest and most profound features of quantum mechanics, famously described by Einstein as "spooky action at a distance." It's a connection between quantum particles that persists no matter how far apart they are. The amount of this connection is measured by a quantity called **entanglement entropy**. But how does this relate to gravity and black holes? The AdS/CFT correspondence provides a stunningly simple geometric answer, known as the Ryu-Takayanagi formula.

This simulation visualizes this connection. We start with the same scenario as the last simulation: a quantum system on the boundary being "quenched" (energized), which corresponds to a shell of energy falling in and forming a black hole in the bulk. We select a region on the boundary (the length of the arc at the top). The entanglement entropy of this region with the rest of the system is, according to the holographic principle, equal to the area of the **minimal surface** in the bulk that ends on the edges of our chosen region. In our 2D view, this is the length of the purple geodesic line.

As the simulation runs, you can see this minimal surface change. Initially, it stays near the boundary. As the black hole forms, the surface is "pulled" deeper into the bulk, and its length grows. The plot on the right tracks this length—the entanglement entropy—over time. It shows a linear growth followed by saturation. This is a key result, suggesting that entanglement spreads through the quantum system at a finite speed, a phenomenon sometimes called an "entanglement tsunami." You are watching entanglement grow by measuring the geometry of an extra dimension.

Core Theory: The Ryu-Takayanagi Formula and Entanglement Growth

The Ryu-Takayanagi (RT) formula, and its covariant generalization by Hubeny, Rangamani, and Takayanagi (HRT), is a cornerstone of the AdS/CFT correspondence. It proposes a direct link between a quantum information quantity on the boundary and a geometric quantity in the bulk. For a given spatial region `A` in the boundary CFT, the entanglement entropy `S_A` is given by:
S_A = Area(γ_A) / (4G_N)
where `γ_A` is the extremal-area codimension-2 surface in the bulk whose boundary matches the boundary of `A` (`∂γ_A = ∂A`). For a static 2D slice of AdS as in our visualization, this simplifies to finding the shortest-length geodesic connecting the endpoints of the region `A`.

This simulation studies the evolution of `S_A` after a global quantum quench, modeled by the collapsing AdS-Vaidya shell. The process is as follows:

  1. At `t=0`, the spacetime is empty AdS. The minimal surface `γ_A` is a simple semicircle that stays near the boundary. The entanglement entropy is small.
  2. The quench injects energy, modeled by the infalling shell. As the shell falls, the geometry inside it changes to that of a black hole.
  3. The minimal surface `γ_A` must now navigate this new, dynamic geometry. To minimize its length, it has to "fall" along with the shell. The tip of the geodesic probes deeper into the bulk as time goes on.
  4. Because the metric "stretches" space near the center of AdS (the factor of `1/z²` in the Poincaré metric `ds² = (dz²+dx²)/z²`), a surface that goes deeper into the bulk has a longer proper length. This causes the entanglement entropy `S_A` to grow.
  5. The growth is found to be linear in time, `S_A(t) ~ v_E * t`, until the surface reaches the newly formed black hole horizon. At this point, it can no longer go deeper, and the entropy saturates at a final thermal value.
This characteristic linear growth and saturation of entanglement entropy is a generic feature of thermalization in chaotic quantum systems and has been a major area of research, with the holographic picture providing key insights. The simulation finds the minimal surface at each time step using a numerical relaxation method, where a chain of points is iteratively adjusted to minimize its total length in the evolving spacetime.
