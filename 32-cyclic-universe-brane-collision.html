Beginner's Guide: The Cosmic Bounce

The Ekpyrotic model provides an alternative to the Big Bang singularity, but what if the collision wasn't a one-time event? The Cyclic Universe model extends this idea by proposing that the brane collisions happen over and over again in an endless cycle of cosmic death and rebirth. This simulation visualizes one such cycle.

As before, the vertical direction is the extra dimension, and the purple line is our universe (a "brane"). We have added a potential energy between the branes, much like the force between two atoms. They attract each other from a distance, but strongly repel if they get too close. The simulation starts with the branes moving apart after a previous collision. The expansion of our universe is shown by the purple line getting wider. As the branes move apart, the attractive force slows them down until they reach a maximum separation. Then, they begin to fall towards each other again. Our universe contracts, heading for a "Big Crunch." But instead of a singularity, the repulsive force causes them to "bounce," triggering a new Big Bang and starting the cycle all over again. The plot on the right shows the size of our universe (the "scale factor") over time, revealing this endless series of bounces.

Core Theory: Inter-Brane Potentials and a Bouncing Cosmology

The Cyclic Model, proposed by Paul Steinhardt and Neil Turok, is a modification of the earlier Ekpyrotic scenario. It relies on a specific potential `V(φ)` between the two branes, where `φ` is the "radion" field representing their separation in the extra dimension. To achieve a cyclic solution, this potential must have specific properties:

  • It must be attractive at large separations to cause the branes to re-collapse after they move apart.
  • It must become strongly repulsive at very short separations to ensure the branes "bounce" rather than annihilate or form a singularity.
  • The mechanism must solve the "entropy problem": in each cycle, entropy is generated, so the universe should get bigger and flatter with each bounce. The model handles this by having the expansion phase be long enough to dilute the entropy from the previous cycle.

This simulation models this dynamic using a classical analogy: a **Lennard-Jones potential**, commonly used to describe the interaction between neutral atoms. The force between the branes is derived from a potential `V(y) = A/y¹² - B/y⁶`, where `y` is the separation. The `1/y¹²` term is a powerful short-range repulsion (the bounce), while the `-1/y⁶` term is a weaker, long-range attraction (the re-collapse). The simulation solves the equation of motion for the brane moving in this potential, `d²y/dt² = -dV/dy`, using a stable Verlet integrator.

A key feature of the model is the connection between the inter-brane distance `y` and the scale factor `a` of the universe on our brane. In many of these models, `a ∝ y^c` for some power `c`. This simulation visualizes this directly by making the width of the purple line proportional to its height `y`. As the branes move apart, our universe expands. As they approach for a collision, our universe contracts. The plot of `a(t)` therefore shows a "bouncing cosmology," avoiding a singularity and suggesting a universe that could be infinitely old and eternally cyclical.
