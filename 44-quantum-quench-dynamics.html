Exposition: Disturbing the Quantum Vacuum

Beginner's Guide: Ringing the Quantum Bell

Imagine a perfectly still bell. This is like a quantum system in its "ground state"—its state of lowest possible energy. What happens if you strike the bell? It rings, vibrating in a complex pattern that slowly fades away. A **quantum quench** is the quantum mechanical equivalent of striking the bell. We start with a system in its stable ground state, and then instantly and violently change one of its fundamental properties (like the strength of a magnetic field). The system is no longer in a stable state and begins to evolve in a highly non-trivial way, exploring its complex quantum nature.

This simulation lets you perform such a quench on a chain of quantum spins. You first prepare the system in its ground state for an "Initial Field." Then, you "Run Quench," which instantly changes the field to a "Final Field" and lets the system evolve. The top plot shows the **magnetization** of the system, which is like the main "tone" of the ringing bell. The bottom plot shows the **entanglement entropy**, a measure of how "quantum" the system is. You can see that after the quench, the system starts to ring (the magnetization oscillates), and at the same time, it becomes much more quantum and complex (the entanglement grows).

Core Theory: Quenching Across a Quantum Phase Transition

This simulation explores the **1D transverse-field Ising model (TFIM)**, the simplest model that exhibits a quantum phase transition. Its Hamiltonian is:
H = -J Σ SᵢᶻSᵢ₊₁ᶻ - g Σ Sᵢˣ
The first term (`J`) wants adjacent spins to align in the z-direction (a ferromagnet). The second term (`g`) is a transverse magnetic field that wants all spins to point in the x-direction (a paramagnet). These two terms compete. At zero temperature, the system undergoes a **quantum phase transition** at the critical point `g/J = 1`.

  • For `g < 1`, the system is in a **ferromagnetic phase**, with a non-zero magnetization `⟨Mᶻ⟩`.
  • For `g > 1`, it's in a **paramagnetic phase**, where `⟨Mᶻ⟩ = 0`.

A quantum quench is a protocol where the system is prepared in the ground state `|ψ₀⟩` of an initial Hamiltonian `H(gᵢ)`, and then evolved in time with a final Hamiltonian `H(g_f)`. Because `|ψ₀⟩` is not an eigenstate of `H(g_f)`, it undergoes non-trivial time evolution. Quenching across the critical point (e.g., from `gᵢ > 1` to `g_f < 1`) is particularly interesting, as it creates a high-energy state with complex relaxation dynamics. The subsequent evolution of observables like the magnetization `⟨Mᶻ(t)⟩` reveals how the system settles, while the growth of entanglement shows how quantum correlations spread through the system.

Advanced Details: Entanglement Growth and Ground State Preparation

After a quench, a key feature of many-body dynamics is the **linear growth of entanglement entropy**. Entanglement entropy measures how much quantum information is shared between two halves of the system. Starting from a low-entanglement ground state, the quench acts as a source of entangled "quasi-particle" pairs that propagate across the system, causing the entanglement between the two halves to grow linearly with time until it saturates at a value determined by the system size. Due to the extreme computational difficulty of calculating true entanglement entropy (requiring a Singular Value Decomposition of the state tensor), this simulation plots a simpler-to-calculate proxy: the **participation entropy** in the computational basis, `S = -Σ |cᵢ|² log(|cᵢ|²)` where `cᵢ` are the state vector amplitudes. This quantity also measures the delocalization of the state in Hilbert space and shows similar qualitative behavior of growth and saturation.

To perform the quench, we must first find the ground state of the initial Hamiltonian `H(gᵢ)`. This simulation achieves this via **imaginary time evolution**. By evolving any random starting state according to the Schrödinger equation with time replaced by `t → -iτ`, `|ψ(τ)⟩ = exp(-Hτ)|ψ(0)⟩`, all higher-energy components are exponentially suppressed relative to the ground state. After a sufficient number of steps, the state vector converges to the true ground state of `H(gᵢ)`, ready for the quench.

Interactive Elements Guide

This is a two-step simulation. You must first prepare the initial state, then run the quench.

  1. Set Initial/Final Fields: Use the sliders to choose the initial transverse field `gᵢ` and the final field `g_f`.
  2. Press "1. Prepare Ground State": This calculates the lowest-energy state for the Hamiltonian with field `gᵢ`. The button will be disabled and the status will update. This may take a few seconds. When it's done, the "Run Quench" button will become active.
  3. Press "2. Run Quench": This starts the time evolution. The system will now evolve according to the Hamiltonian with the final field `g_f`, and the plots will update in real time.

Interesting Quenches to Try:

  • Quench within the Paramagnetic Phase: Set `gᵢ = 3.0` and `g_f = 2.0`. Prepare and run. The magnetization stays at zero, but you will see a small amount of entanglement generated.
  • Quench within the Ferromagnetic Phase: Set `gᵢ = 0.2` and `g_f = 0.6`. Prepare and run. The magnetization will oscillate around a non-zero value.
  • Quench Across the Critical Point (Para → Ferro): This is the most dramatic. Set `gᵢ = 2.5` (paramagnet) and `g_f = 0.5` (ferromagnet). Prepare and run. You will see large oscillations in the magnetization as the system tries to decide which way to align (up or down), and a very rapid, linear growth in entanglement.
  • Quench to the Critical Point: Set `gᵢ = 2.0` and `g_f = 1.0`. Quenching to the critical point often results in the slowest relaxation dynamics.
