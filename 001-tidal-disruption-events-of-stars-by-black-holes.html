    Overview for Lay Readers

    Imagine a star, much like our Sun, wandering too close to a colossal black hole lurking in the center of a galaxy. This isn't just any black hole; it's a supermassive one, millions or even billions of times heavier than our Sun. As the star approaches, the black hole's immense gravity pulls on it. The pull is so much stronger on the side of the star closer to the black hole than on the far side. This difference in gravitational force, known as a tidal force, stretches the star. At a certain point, this stretching force overwhelms the star's own gravity that holds it together. The star is torn apart in a catastrophic event called a Tidal Disruption Event (TDE). Its stellar gas is pulled into long, spaghetti-like streams that then orbit the black hole, forming a glowing, super-hot disk of material. This process releases a brilliant flare of light across the electromagnetic spectrum, from X-rays to radio waves, which can outshine the entire galaxy for a short time. This simulation allows you to witness a simplified version of this cosmic drama, control the key parameters, and explore the physics behind one of the most violent events in the universe.

    Core Theory of Tidal Disruption Events

    A Tidal Disruption Event (TDE) is governed by a contest between the self-gravity of a star and the tidal forces of a supermassive black hole (SMBH). The fundamental physics can be understood by analyzing these two forces.

    The Tidal Radius (Roche Limit)

    The key concept is the tidal radius, often called the Roche limit for a fluid body. This is the distance from the central body (the black hole) at which the tidal forces pulling an orbiting body (the star) apart are equal to the self-gravity holding the star together. If a star passes within this radius, it will be disrupted.

    Let's derive it. Consider a star of mass M* and radius R* at a distance d from a black hole of mass MBH. A small mass m on the surface of the star closest to the black hole is at distance d - R*, and a similar mass on the far side is at d + R*. The black hole's gravitational acceleration is given by a = GMBH/r2.

    The differential force (tidal force) across the star is the difference in the gravitational force on the near and far sides:

    ΔFtidal ≈ |(dF/dr)| * (2R*) = (2GMBH/d3) * m * (2R*) - this is an approximation. A more accurate calculation considers the differential acceleration:

    Δatidal = anear - afar = GMBH/(d - R*)2 - GMBH/(d + R*)2

    For d >> R*, we can use a Taylor expansion: (d ± R*)-2 ≈ d-2(1 ∓ 2R*/d). This gives:

    Δatidal ≈ (GMBH/d2) * [(1 + 2R*/d) - (1 - 2R*/d)] = 4GMBHR*/d3.

    The self-gravity of the star holding the mass m to it is approximately aself = GM*/R*2.

    Disruption occurs when the tidal acceleration exceeds the self-gravity acceleration. The tidal radius RT is where they are equal:

    GM*/R*2 = 2GMBHR*/RT3 (Note: The factor of 2 is a common simplification; the exact factor depends on the star's structure).

    Solving for RT gives the classic formula:

    RT ≈ R* (MBH/M*)1/3

    This simple formula is remarkably powerful. It tells us that more massive black holes have larger tidal radii, but the dependence is weak (cube root). Denser stars (smaller R* for a given M*) have smaller tidal radii and are harder to disrupt.

    The Impact Parameter and Penetration Factor

    A star does not need to hit the tidal radius head-on. It typically follows a parabolic or hyperbolic orbit. The key parameter describing the closeness of the encounter is the **impact parameter (b)**, the perpendicular distance between the black hole and the star's initial trajectory. In the simulation, we often use the **periapsis distance (Rp)**, which is the distance of closest approach.

    For a parabolic orbit, Rp is related to the star's initial velocity at infinity, but in our simulation, we set it directly. The degree of disruption is quantified by the **penetration factor, β = RT / Rp**.

    • β < 1: Grazing encounter. The star is outside the tidal radius at its closest approach. It gets distorted and loses some mass but largely survives.
    • β ≈ 1: Partial disruption. The star is partially disrupted, losing a significant fraction of its mass.
    • β > 1: Full disruption. The star is completely torn apart.
    • β >> 1: Deep plunge. The star passes deep inside the tidal radius, leading to a more violent and rapid disruption.

    The Fate of the Debris

    After the star is disrupted, its constituent gas forms long streams. The dynamics of this debris are governed by Keplerian orbits around the black hole, but with a spread of energies and angular momenta inherited from the star's original motion and size.

    An elegant result from theory, first shown by Rees (1988), is that the specific energy spread of the debris is approximately:

    ΔE ≈ (GMBHR*)/RT2

    Since the star was initially on a nearly parabolic orbit (total energy ≈ 0), the disrupted gas will have a range of energies centered around zero.

    • Negative Energy Debris (E < 0): This material is gravitationally bound to the black hole. It will fall back towards the periapsis on elliptical orbits.
    • Positive Energy Debris (E > 0): This material is unbound and will escape the black hole's gravity on hyperbolic trajectories.

    A remarkable consequence is that roughly **half the star's mass becomes bound**, and the other half is unbound and ejected. You can verify this in the simulation by watching the "Bound Mass Fraction" metric after a full disruption.

    Accretion and the Flare

    The bound debris does not fall into the black hole immediately. Due to its angular momentum, it orbits the black hole. As the streams of gas return to the periapsis, they collide with themselves, shocking the gas, dissipating orbital energy, and causing the debris to settle into a circular accretion disk.

    The rate at which mass falls back to the periapsis region can be calculated from the distribution of orbital periods of the bound debris. The orbital period P is related to the semi-major axis a by Kepler's third law: P2 ∝ a3. The energy E is related to a by E = -GMBH/(2a). Combining these gives a relationship between fallback time and the energy distribution.

    The result is a characteristic mass fallback rate that follows a power-law decay with time:

    Ṁ(t) ∝ t-5/3

    This is a hallmark prediction of TDE theory. As this material accretes onto the black hole, its gravitational potential energy is converted into radiation, producing the bright flare. The luminosity of the flare is expected to track this fallback rate, so we also expect L(t) ∝ t-5/3 after an initial rise. Our simulation charts this predicted fallback rate.

    Direct Capture and Relativistic Effects

    For a star to be tidally disrupted, the tidal radius RT must be outside the black hole's event horizon. The radius of the event horizon (the Schwarzschild radius) is RS = 2GMBH/c2.

    If RT < RS, the star will be swallowed whole by the black hole before tidal forces can rip it apart. This creates a condition on the black hole mass for which TDEs are visible. By setting RT = RS for a Sun-like star, we can find this limit:

    R* (MBH/M*)1/3 = 2GMBH/c2

    Solving for MBH gives a maximum mass of roughly 108 solar masses. Above this mass, SMBHs tend to swallow Sun-like stars whole, producing no bright flare. This is why TDEs are typically observed from SMBHs in the range of 106 to 108 solar masses. The simulation includes a warning if your parameters enter this "direct capture" regime.

    Furthermore, for the most massive black holes or deepest encounters, General Relativity becomes important. The periapsis of the debris orbits will precess, and the simple Keplerian picture breaks down. This simulation uses Newtonian gravity but includes diagnostics that hint at when GR effects would become significant (e.g., when Rp is close to RS).

    Worked Examples

    Example 1: Calculating the Tidal Radius

    Problem: A Sun-like star (M* = 1 M☉, R* = 1 R☉) approaches a 106 M☉ supermassive black hole. Calculate its tidal radius.

    Given:

    • MBH = 106 M☉
    • M* = 1 M☉
    • R* = 1 R☉ ≈ 7 x 108 m

    Formula: RT ≈ R* (MBH/M*)1/3

    Calculation:

    1. Calculate the mass ratio: MBH/M* = 106 / 1 = 106.
    2. Take the cube root of the mass ratio: (106)1/3 = 100.
    3. Multiply by the star's radius: RT ≈ 100 * R* = 100 * (7 x 108 m) = 7 x 1010 m.

    Answer: The tidal radius is approximately 100 solar radii, or 70 million kilometers. For comparison, the distance from the Sun to Mercury is about 58 million kilometers. So, the star would be disrupted if it came closer to the black hole than the orbit of Mercury is to our Sun.

    Example 2: Determining the Outcome

    Problem: The star from Example 1 has a parabolic trajectory with a periapsis distance (closest approach) of Rp = 5 x 1010 m. Will it be disrupted?

    Given:

    • RT = 7 x 1010 m (from previous example)
    • Rp = 5 x 1010 m

    Formula: Penetration factor β = RT / Rp.

    Calculation:

    1. β = (7 x 1010 m) / (5 x 1010 m) = 1.4.

    Answer: Since β = 1.4 > 1, the star passes well inside its tidal radius. It will be fully and violently disrupted. Try setting these parameters in the simulation using the "Custom" preset to see this happen.

    Example 3: Estimating the Fallback Time

    Problem: For the disruption in Example 2, what is the orbital period of the most tightly bound debris? This sets the timescale for the flare to rise.

    Theory: The most tightly bound debris has a specific energy of approximately -ΔE, where ΔE ≈ GMBHR*/RT2. The semi-major axis a is given by E = -GMBH/(2a), so a ≈ GMBH/(2ΔE) = RT2/(2R*). The fallback time tmin is half the period of this orbit (since it starts at apoapsis).

    Formula: tmin = (1/2) * 2π * sqrt(a3/(GMBH)) = π * sqrt(a3/(GMBH))

    Calculation:

    1. Calculate semi-major axis a: a ≈ RT2/(2R*) = (100 R*)2 / (2R*) = 5000 R*. a = 5000 * (7 x 108 m) = 3.5 x 1012 m.
    2. Calculate GMBH: G ≈ 6.674 x 10-11 N(m/kg)2. MBH = 106 * (2 x 1030 kg) = 2 x 1036 kg. GMBH ≈ 1.33 x 1026 m3/s2.
    3. Calculate period: tmin = π * sqrt((3.5 x 1012)3 / (1.33 x 1026)) ≈ 3.2 x 106 s.

    Answer: The fallback time is about 3.2 million seconds, which is roughly 37 days. This is a typical timescale for TDE flares to rise to their peak brightness. The simulation's "Time to Peak Fallback" metric calculates this value for you.

    Many Analogies for TDEs

    • Spaghettification: This is the classic analogy. Imagine holding a piece of dough and pulling on one end much more strongly than the other. The dough will stretch out into a long, thin noodle. The black hole's gravity does the same to the star, stretching it into a cosmic spaghetti strand.
    • The School of Fish: Think of the star as a tightly packed school of fish, all swimming together due to their mutual attraction (gravity). A massive shark (the black hole) swims by. The fish closest to the shark are pulled much more strongly than the fish on the other side of the school. The school is pulled apart, with some fish captured by the shark's wake and others scattered away.
    • The Sandcastle at High Tide: You build a sandcastle (the star) on the beach. Its own weight and the wetness of the sand hold it together (self-gravity). As the tide comes in (the star approaches the black hole), the water exerts a force. The water level difference between the front and back of the castle creates a pressure gradient that undermines its structure, causing it to collapse and wash away.
    • A Space Station in a Gravity Gradient: Imagine a long space station orbiting a planet. Astronauts at the end closer to the planet feel a slightly stronger gravitational pull than the station's center, while those at the far end feel a weaker pull. This creates a constant stretching force along the station's length. For a star near a black hole, this "gravity gradient" is so extreme it rips the object apart.
    • The Comet Breakup: We've seen this in our own solar system with comets like Shoemaker-Levy 9, which was torn into a "string of pearls" by Jupiter's tidal forces before crashing into the planet. A TDE is a much more extreme version of the same physical process, with a star instead of a comet and a supermassive black hole instead of Jupiter.

    Intuition & Visual Metaphors

    To build intuition, let's focus on the energy of the stellar debris. Before disruption, every piece of the star is on roughly the same orbit, with a total energy near zero. When the star is at periapsis (closest approach), it's moving very fast. The part of the star closer to the black hole gets an extra "kick" from the gravity, boosting its speed, while the part farther away gets a smaller kick.

    Visualize the star's gas as runners in a race. They all start together. As they round a sharp, banked corner (the black hole), the runners on the inside lane have to travel a shorter path and get a stronger gravitational slingshot. They come out of the turn with much more energy and speed, enough to escape the stadium entirely (unbound debris). The runners on the outside lane have a longer path and get a weaker slingshot; they don't have enough speed to escape and fall back towards the starting line (bound debris). The runners in the exact middle lane end up roughly where they started. This is why the debris energy distribution is centered around zero, leading to the 50/50 split between bound and unbound mass.

    Another key visual is the **"pancake" effect**. As the star approaches the black hole, it's not just stretched along the direction to the black hole; it's also compressed vertically. This is because the tidal forces are directed towards the center of the star in the perpendicular directions. So, the star is first squeezed into a pancake shape in its orbital plane before being shredded into a noodle. This pancake compression can trigger nuclear reactions in the star's core even before disruption, leading to a "pre-flare" signature that astronomers are searching for.

    Advanced Extensions

    • Spinning Black Holes (Kerr Black Holes): Our simulation assumes a non-spinning (Schwarzschild) black hole. If the black hole is spinning, it drags spacetime around with it (Lense-Thirring effect). This drastically changes the orbits of the debris, leading to nodal precession and potentially making disruptions more or less likely depending on the orientation of the star's orbit relative to the black hole's spin. For prograde orbits (star orbits in the same direction as the spin), the star can get closer before disruption, increasing the chances of direct capture.
    • Stellar Structure and Composition: We model the star as a simple fluid. In reality, stars have complex internal structures (core, envelope) and compositions. A dense, compact white dwarf will have a much smaller tidal radius than a fluffy red giant of the same mass. Disrupting a white dwarf is much harder and happens much closer to the black hole, where GR effects are dominant. The resulting flare would be very different.
    • Reprocessing and Radiative Transfer: The initial flare from the accretion disk is primarily in extreme UV and X-rays. However, this intense radiation illuminates the vast, unbound debris streams. This outflowing gas can absorb the high-energy photons and re-radiate them at lower energies, such as in the optical and UV bands. Much of what we observe as a TDE's light is this "reprocessed" emission, which complicates the interpretation of the light curve.
    • Magnetic Fields and Jets: If the original star was magnetized, or if magnetic fields are generated in the accretion disk (via the Magneto-Rotational Instability, or MRI), they can have profound effects. Strong, ordered magnetic fields threading the black hole can launch powerful relativistic jets of plasma, similar to those seen in quasars. The event ASASSN-14li is a famous TDE where evidence for a jet was found.
    • Partially-Bound Debris and Late-Time Behavior: The simple t-5/3 model assumes all bound debris eventually accretes. However, some debris can be on very long, eccentric orbits that take thousands of years to return. Furthermore, the newly formed accretion disk itself can be eccentric and precess, leading to quasi-periodic modulations in the late-time light curve of the TDE.

    Q&A Section

    1. What is a tidal force, really?

    A tidal force is not a fundamental force of nature; it's a differential gravitational force. Gravity weakens with distance. For a large object like a star near a black hole, the part of the star closer to the black hole is pulled significantly more strongly than the part farther away. This difference in pull across the object creates a stretching force. On Earth, the Moon's tidal force does the same thing to our oceans, pulling the water on the near side toward it and pulling the Earth away from the water on the far side, creating two high tides. For a star near a black hole, this force is millions of times stronger and can exceed the star's own gravity.

    2. Why is the mass fallback rate proportional to t-5/3?

    This famous result comes from combining Kepler's laws with the energy distribution of the debris. The key steps are: 1) The mass dM with orbital periods between P and P+dP is related to the mass with energies between E and E+dE. Assuming a uniform energy distribution (a decent approximation), dM/dE is constant. 2) Kepler's third law relates period and semi-major axis 'a': P ∝ a3/2. 3) The orbital energy is E = -GMBH/(2a). From this, we can find the relationship between P and E: a ∝ -1/E, so P ∝ (-E)-3/2. 4) Differentiating gives dP/dE ∝ (-E)-5/2. 5) The mass fallback rate Ṁ is dM/dt, which is the same as dM/dP (since P is time). Ṁ = dM/dP = (dM/dE) * (dE/dP). Since dM/dE is constant, Ṁ ∝ dE/dP ∝ (-E)5/2. 6) Finally, since P ∝ (-E)-3/2, we have (-E) ∝ P-2/3. Substituting this into our expression for Ṁ gives Ṁ ∝ (P-2/3)5/2 = P-5/3. Since time t is just the period P, we get Ṁ(t) ∝ t-5/3.

    3. Can we see TDEs with telescopes?

    Yes, absolutely! TDEs are a hot topic in transient astronomy. Observatories performing all-sky surveys, like the Zwicky Transient Facility (ZTF), Pan-STARRS, and the upcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory, are finding dozens of TDE candidates per year. They are identified as bright, transient flares of light coming from the centers of otherwise dormant galaxies. Follow-up observations with telescopes like Swift (for UV/X-ray), Hubble (for high-resolution imaging), and VLT/Keck (for spectroscopy) are crucial to confirm their nature and study the physics. The characteristic t-5/3 light curve decay and spectral signatures (broad hydrogen and helium lines) are key identifiers.

    4. What happens to the half of the star that is unbound?

    The unbound debris, carrying positive orbital energy, is ejected from the system on hyperbolic trajectories. It forms a massive, expanding cloud of gas moving at speeds up to several percent of the speed of light (10,000 km/s). This material travels out into the host galaxy, eventually mixing with the interstellar medium. This is one way that galaxies can be enriched with heavier elements that were forged inside the disrupted star.

    5. How accurate is this simulation?

    This simulation is a simplified but powerful educational tool. It uses Newtonian gravity and models the star as a collection of non-interacting particles (after disruption), which is a "ballistic" approximation. Real TDEs involve complex gas dynamics (hydrodynamics), magnetic fields (MHD), and general relativity. For instance, we don't simulate the debris stream shocking and circularizing to form a disk; we infer it from the fallback rate. However, the core physics—the tidal disruption condition, the parabolic trajectories, the energy spread of the debris, and the t-5/3 fallback—are captured remarkably well by this simplified model and provide a solid foundation for understanding the phenomenon.

    6. Does the star's own spin matter?

    Yes, the star's rotation can have a noticeable effect. A rapidly spinning star is already slightly flattened at its poles and bulging at its equator, making it less gravitationally bound than a non-spinning star of the same mass. This makes it easier to disrupt (i.e., its tidal radius is effectively larger). The orientation of the star's spin axis relative to its orbit can also introduce asymmetries in the debris stream, potentially affecting the shape of the resulting accretion disk and the light curve of the flare.

    7. How long does a TDE flare last?

    The timescale of a TDE is set by the dynamics of the fallback debris. As we calculated in the worked example, the flare typically rises to a peak over a period of a month to a few months. The subsequent decay phase, following the t-5/3 law, can last for several months to a few years before the TDE fades back into obscurity. Some TDEs have shown late-time re-brightening or plateaus, suggesting more complex processes are at play in the accretion disk.

    8. Could a TDE happen in our own galaxy?

    Yes, and it has likely happened many times over the Milky Way's history. Our galaxy's central supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*), has a mass of about 4 million solar masses, which is ideal for causing TDEs. Based on the rate of TDEs seen in other galaxies, astronomers estimate that a TDE should occur in a Milky Way-like galaxy roughly once every 10,000 to 100,000 years. We are not currently aware of any star on a trajectory that would lead to a TDE in the near future, but the galactic center is a very crowded place, so it's a statistical certainty over long timescales.

    9. What is the difference between a TDE and a supernova?

    Both are brilliant cosmic explosions, but their mechanisms are completely different. A supernova is the death of a single, massive star (or a white dwarf in a binary system) caused by internal processes—either its core collapses under its own gravity or it undergoes runaway nuclear fusion. A TDE is an external process where a star, which could have otherwise lived for billions of years, is destroyed by the gravity of a black hole. Spectroscopically, they also look very different. Supernovae show spectral lines indicating matter moving at high speeds in a roughly spherical explosion, often rich in heavy elements. TDEs show lines of hydrogen and helium from the star's gas orbiting a central object, and their light curves have a very different shape (t-5/3 decay vs. the radioactive decay-powered curve of a supernova).

    10. Can a star be partially disrupted and survive?

    Yes. If the star's periapsis distance Rp is just slightly less than the tidal radius RT (a penetration factor β slightly greater than 1, or even slightly less), it may only lose its outer layers to the black hole. The gravitationally bound core of the star would remain intact. This "partially disrupted" star would be kicked into a new orbit, having lost mass and gained a significant velocity kick. It would appear as a hypervelocity star that is unusually poor in hydrogen (since it lost its outer envelope). Finding such an object would be strong evidence of a past partial TDE.

    11. How much energy is released in a TDE?

    A tremendous amount. The total energy radiated can be on the order of 1044 to 1046 Joules (1051 to 1053 ergs). This is comparable to, and can sometimes exceed, the energy of a typical supernova. This energy comes from the conversion of gravitational potential energy of the accreting gas into heat and light. The efficiency of this conversion (the η in L=ηṀc²) is typically around 10% for a standard accretion disk, meaning 10% of the rest mass energy of the accreted material is released as radiation. Since several solar masses of material can accrete, the energy output is immense.

    12. What if the star is part of a binary system?

    This creates a fascinating scenario. If a binary star system has a close encounter with an SMBH, one star might be captured and disrupted while the other is ejected at high speed, becoming a hypervelocity star. A famous mechanism proposed by Jack Hills involves a binary getting scattered by the SMBH. The tidal forces of the black hole break the binary apart. One star is captured into a tight orbit around the SMBH, while its companion is ejected from the galaxy entirely. This is a leading theory for the origin of the hypervelocity stars we observe.

    13. Do all TDEs look the same?

    No, there is a surprising diversity. Some TDEs are very bright in X-rays ("X-ray TDEs"), others are bright in optical/UV light ("optical TDEs"), and some are even "radio-loud," producing jets. This diversity is thought to be driven by several factors: the mass of the black hole, the penetration factor β, the type of star disrupted, the viewing angle of the observer relative to the accretion disk/outflow, and the presence of magnetic fields. Understanding this "TDE zoo" is a major goal of current research.

    14. Can a black hole disrupt something other than a star?

    Yes. The same tidal physics applies to any object. A giant molecular cloud could be shredded and consumed, a process that might feed the black hole over a long period. A planet, asteroid, or comet could also be tidally disrupted, though the resulting flare would be much fainter and harder to detect than from a full-sized star. There is ongoing debate about whether some smaller, faster flares from galactic centers could be due to the disruption of white dwarfs or giant planets.

    15. What is the "Pancake Detonation" scenario?

    This is a more exotic TDE theory. As mentioned in the "Intuition" section, a star is compressed vertically into a "pancake" at periapsis before being stretched. For very deep encounters (β >> 1), this compression can be so intense that it triggers explosive nuclear burning (detonation) in the star's core. This could cause the star to explode like a supernova *at the same time* it's being tidally disrupted. This "pancake detonation" would produce unique observational signatures, including specific heavy elements forged in the explosion, which astronomers are actively searching for.

    16. Why is the unbound debris energy positive?

    An object's total orbital energy is the sum of its kinetic energy (positive) and its gravitational potential energy (negative). An object is bound if its total energy is negative (potential > kinetic), on a parabolic "escape" trajectory if its energy is exactly zero, and unbound if its energy is positive (kinetic > potential). The debris from the disrupted star inherits a range of energies. The material that gets a large velocity boost from the close passage ends up with enough kinetic energy to overcome the negative potential energy, resulting in a net positive total energy. This allows it to fly away from the black hole forever.

    17. How do we measure the black hole's mass from a TDE?

    It's challenging but possible. Several methods can be used to estimate MBH. 1) If the host galaxy's properties (like its stellar velocity dispersion) are known, we can use established scaling relations (like the M-sigma relation) to estimate the black hole's mass. 2) The total radiated energy of the flare is proportional to the accreted mass, which is about half the star's mass. By assuming a star type (e.g., 1 solar mass), we can estimate the radiated energy and work backward to get the black hole mass. 3) The peak luminosity of the flare is thought to be related to the Eddington luminosity, which is directly proportional to MBH. 4) The rise time of the flare is also dependent on MBH (as seen in our worked example). By combining these clues, astronomers can get a rough estimate of the mass of the previously dormant black hole.

    18. What is the "Eddington Luminosity" and why is it important?

    The Eddington Luminosity (LEdd) is a critical concept in accretion physics. It's the maximum luminosity a body (like a star or accretion disk) can achieve while in hydrostatic equilibrium. At this luminosity, the outward force of radiation pressure on the accreting gas exactly balances the inward pull of gravity. If the luminosity were to exceed LEdd, the radiation would blow away the accreting gas, shutting off its own fuel supply. For a black hole of mass MBH, LEdd ≈ 1.3 x 1031 * (MBH/M☉) Watts. Many TDEs have peak luminosities close to the Eddington limit for their inferred black hole mass, suggesting that the accretion rate is regulated by this process.

    19. Can TDEs create gravitational waves?

    Yes, but they are very difficult to detect with current instruments like LIGO/Virgo. Gravitational waves are generated by accelerating asymmetric masses. The process of a star being stretched and swung around a black hole does produce them. However, the frequencies of these waves are typically in the milliHertz to microHertz range, far too low for ground-based detectors. The future space-based observatory LISA (Laser Interferometer Space Antenna) will be sensitive to these low frequencies and should be able to detect the gravitational waves from TDEs, especially those involving white dwarfs or intermediate-mass black holes, opening a completely new window onto these events.

    20. How is the resolution control in the simulation implemented?

    The "Resolution" slider controls the number of particles (N) used to represent the star. A higher resolution (more particles) provides a smoother and more accurate representation of the stellar fluid's dynamics, especially the fine details of the tidal streams. However, the computational cost scales with the number of particles. In this simulation, the gravitational force on each particle from the black hole must be calculated at each time step. The total number of calculations per step is proportional to N. Therefore, doubling the number of particles roughly doubles the CPU load. The slider allows you to find a balance between visual fidelity and smooth performance on your specific hardware. A "Heavy" setting might use N=500 particles, while "Light" might use N=50.
