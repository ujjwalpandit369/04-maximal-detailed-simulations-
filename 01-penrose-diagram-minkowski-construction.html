Beginner's Guide: From Spacetime to Penrose Diagrams

A Penrose diagram is essentially a map of spacetime that has been "squashed" to fit infinity into a finite picture. It's a tool that lets physicists see the entire causal structure of a spacetime at a glance. This page guides you through creating the simplest one: for the flat, empty spacetime of Special Relativity (Minkowski space).

Stage 1: The Standard Spacetime Diagram

We start with a normal spacetime diagram. The vertical axis is time (t) and the horizontal axis is one dimension of space (x). A point on this diagram is an "event." The path of an object through spacetime is its "worldline." Nothing can travel faster than light, so worldlines of massive particles must always have a slope steeper than 45 degrees. Light itself travels at exactly 45 degrees. These 45-degree lines form a "light cone" at every event, defining its causal past and future.

Stage 2: Rotating to Null Coordinates

The first step is a change of coordinates. Instead of (t, x), we use "null coordinates" u = t - x and v = t + x. These coordinates are chosen because lines of constant u or constant v represent the paths of light rays. This has the convenient effect of rotating the entire diagram by 45 degrees. Now, light rays travel horizontally and vertically. The grid is no longer made of squares but of diamonds.

Stage 3: Squeezing Infinity with Arctan

This is the magic step. The null coordinates u and v extend from -∞ to +∞. To fit them on a finite page, we apply a mathematical function that maps an infinite range to a finite one. The standard choice is the arctangent function. We define new coordinates U = arctan(u) and V = arctan(v). Since arctan(anything) is always between -π/2 and +π/2, the entire infinite spacetime is now contained within a finite diamond shape. This is a "conformal" transformation, which means it preserves angles locally. Light cones still look like they are at 45 degrees, so the causal structure is preserved. This is the key property that makes Penrose diagrams so useful.

Stage 4: The Final Penrose Diagram

Finally, we rotate the diagram back by 45 degrees for convention, so that the time-like direction is generally "up." We now have the Penrose diagram for Minkowski space. It's a diamond. The corners and edges of this diamond represent the "infinities" of the original spacetime. For example, the right corner, called "spacelike infinity" (i⁰), is where all spacelike lines meet. The top corner, "future timelike infinity" (i⁺), is the destination for all observers moving slower than light. The right edges, "future and past null infinity" (ℐ⁺ and ℐ⁻), are where light rays begin and end.
