From Flat Space to Black Holes

In the previous simulation, we saw how to represent the entirety of infinite, flat Minkowski space in a compact diamond shape. Now, we apply this powerful technique to a much more interesting spacetime: one containing an eternal, non-rotating black hole, as described by the Schwarzschild metric. The resulting Penrose diagram reveals a shocking and bizarre structure that is completely hidden by more conventional coordinate systems. It shows not just a black hole, but also a "white hole," and a separate, parallel universe connected to our own through a non-traversable wormhole (an Einstein-Rosen bridge). This diagram is the key to understanding the true causal structure of this idealized spacetime.

The Problem with Schwarzschild Coordinates

The standard coordinates used to describe a black hole are Schwarzschild coordinates (t, r, θ, φ). In these coordinates, something strange happens at a specific radius, r=2M (where M is the mass of the black hole). This is the event horizon. The metric components either go to zero or infinity here, suggesting a physical singularity. However, this is just a "coordinate singularity"—an artifact of a poor choice of map, like the North and South Poles in a standard Mercator map of the Earth. Physicists showed that you could extend spacetime beyond the horizon by using better coordinates. The Penrose diagram is the ultimate result of this extension.

Kruskal-Szekeres Coordinates: The Key

The breakthrough came with the invention of Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates. These coordinates are well-behaved at the event horizon and reveal the full, "maximally extended" spacetime. The Penrose diagram is simply the Kruskal-Szekeres diagram, conformally squashed into a finite shape, just as we did for Minkowski space. The result is the famous diagram with four distinct regions.

The Four Regions of the Schwarzschild Spacetime

The Penrose diagram for the Schwarzschild black hole is divided into four quadrants, each representing a different region of the spacetime.

  • Region I (The Right Quadrant): "Our Universe" - This is an asymptotically flat region, like our own spacetime far from any gravitational sources. Observers can exist here and send and receive signals from infinity. This is where we would start.
  • Region II (The Top Quadrant): "The Black Hole" - This region is bounded in the past by the future event horizon and in the future by the spacelike singularity. Once an observer or a light ray crosses from Region I into Region II, it is doomed. All future paths, no matter how they accelerate, end at the future singularity (the jagged line at the top). The light cones are tipped so completely towards the singularity that "up" (increasing time) inevitably means "towards r=0".
  • Region III (The Left Quadrant): "Parallel Universe" - This is another, completely separate, asymptotically flat region. It is causally disconnected from Region I. You cannot travel from Region I to Region III, nor can you send a signal. They are connected only through the wormhole's interior.
  • Region IV (The Bottom Quadrant): "The White Hole" - This is the time-reversal of a black hole. It is a region from which objects and light can emerge, but which nothing can enter. It is bounded in the future by the past event horizon and in the past by a past spacelike singularity. It represents a "fountain" of matter and energy. While a valid mathematical solution, white holes are not believed to exist in our universe, as they are thermodynamically unstable.

The horizontal lines separating the regions are the event horizons. The jagged lines at the top and bottom are the future and past singularities, where spacetime curvature becomes infinite and physics as we know it breaks down.
