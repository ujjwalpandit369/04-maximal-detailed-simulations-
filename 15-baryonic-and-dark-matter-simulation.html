Beginner's Guide: The Two Types of Matter

The universe is made of two main types of matter that behave very differently. The first is the normal matter we are made of (protons, electrons, etc.), which astronomers call "baryonic matter." When particles of this matter get close, they collide, stick, and create pressure, like the air in a tire. The second type is "dark matter," a mysterious substance that makes up about 85% of the universe's matter. The crucial difference is that dark matter is "collisionless"—its particles can pass right through each other, interacting only through gravity.

This simulation shows the profound consequences of this difference. We start with two identical, overlapping clouds of particles: dark matter (white) and baryonic gas (yellow). Both clouds feel the same pull of gravity and begin to collapse. You can toggle the visibility of each component with the checkboxes. Watch the dark matter first. It collapses into a diffuse, spread-out "halo." The particles orbit through the center and back out, passing through each other without interacting. Now, watch the gas. It also collapses, but because its particles can collide, they can't just pass through the center. They crash into each other, creating immense pressure and shocking the gas. This pressure allows the gas to shed its orbital energy and settle into a dense, compact disc at the very heart of the dark matter halo. This is why galaxies form where they do: they are the pools of normal matter that have settled at the bottom of the deep gravitational wells created by invisible dark matter halos.

Core Theory: A Hybrid N-Body and Hydrodynamics Approach

This simulation models a two-component system to capture the essential physics of galaxy formation.
1. Dark Matter (Collisionless Component): The white particles are treated as a standard collisionless N-body system. Their motion is governed purely by gravity. They are evolved using a leapfrog integrator under the influence of the total gravitational potential.

2. Baryonic Gas (Fluid Component): The yellow particles represent a simplified gas. They are subject to two forces:

  • The same gravitational force as the dark matter particles.
  • A pressure force, which is calculated using a simplified grid-based hydrodynamics model.
The pressure force is given by `F_p = -∇P`. To model this without a full hydrodynamic solver, we use an "isothermal equation of state," `P = c_s² * ρ_gas`, where `ρ_gas` is the density of the gas and `c_s` is the speed of sound (which you can control with the "Gas Pressure" slider). The simulation calculates `ρ_gas` on a grid, then computes the pressure `P` on that grid, and finally finds the pressure force by taking the numerical gradient of `P`.

The Combined Evolution: The gravitational potential is sourced by the combined density of *both* dark matter and gas. This potential creates the gravitational well. Both components fall into this well. However, the dark matter particles, being collisionless, will orbit within the halo and maintain a large, diffuse configuration (a "virialized halo"). The gas particles, on the other hand, feel the additional pressure force. This force resists compression and allows the gas to dissipate orbital energy through shocks, causing it to lose support and sink to the center of the potential well, where it forms a dense, rotationally supported disk—the progenitor of a galaxy.
