Beginner's Guide: Building Spacetime with Triangles

Instead of spacetime being a smooth sheet, what if it's "built" from tiny, fundamental triangular building blocks? This is the core idea behind Causal Dynamical Triangulations (CDT). This approach to quantum gravity is like building a universe with LEGOs, but the LEGOs are triangles (or their higher-dimensional versions, called simplices), and the building rules are governed by quantum mechanics and causality.

This simulation shows a 1+1 dimensional toy universe being built in this way. We start with a simple, regular arrangement of triangles forming a cylinder. This is just one possible "shape" for the universe. The simulation then explores other possible shapes using a Monte Carlo algorithm. At each step, it randomly picks a location and tries to make a small change to the triangulation—a "move." In this simulation, it performs a "diagonal flip," which takes two adjacent triangles and re-arranges them. This move is either accepted or rejected based on a set of rules derived from Einstein's theory of relativity (the "action"). Over many thousands of steps, the simulation explores a vast number of possible spacetime geometries. The amazing discovery of CDT is that, even though the microscopic rules are very simple and quantum, a stable, large-scale universe with properties like our own (e.g., being 4-dimensional and having a well-behaved shape) can emerge automatically from the collective behavior of these fluctuating triangles.

Core Theory: The CDT Path Integral and Monte Carlo Methods

Causal Dynamical Triangulations defines the quantum gravity path integral (`Z = ∫ D[g] exp(iS[g])`) as a sum over a space of discrete, causally well-behaved geometries. Each geometry, or "spacetime history," is a triangulation `T` built from simplices (in our 2D case, triangles) glued together along their faces. A key feature of CDT is that it imposes a global time foliation, meaning the geometry has a well-defined series of time slices. This distinguishes it from older "Euclidean Dynamical Triangulations" and is crucial for obtaining a well-behaved continuum limit.

The sum over all possible triangulations is weighted by a discrete form of the Einstein-Hilbert action, known as the Regge action. For 2D CDT, this action simplifies remarkably:
S_E = -k₀N₀ + k₂N₂
where `N₀` is the total number of vertices and `N₂` is the total number of triangles in the triangulation. The parameter `k₀` is related to the cosmological constant, and `k₂` is related to the inverse of Newton's constant.

This sum over all `T` is computationally intractable. Instead, we use a Metropolis Monte Carlo algorithm to sample the most important configurations in the sum. The algorithm works as follows:

  1. Start with an initial valid triangulation.
  2. Propose a local change to the triangulation, a "move." This simulation uses the simplest such move, the `(2,2)` or "diagonal flip" move. This move finds two triangles sharing an edge and flips the shared edge to connect the other two vertices.
  3. Calculate the change in the action, `ΔS = S_new - S_old`. For the `(2,2)` move and the simple action above, `N₀` and `N₂` do not change, so `ΔS = 0`.
  4. Accept the move with probability `P = min(1, exp(-ΔS))`. Since `ΔS=0`, all valid `(2,2)` moves are accepted. In a more complex simulation with moves that change `N₀` or `N₂`, this step would be non-trivial.
  5. Repeat millions of times.
By doing this, the simulation generates a sequence of triangulations that are representative of the full quantum sum. We can then measure geometric properties of these configurations to determine the large-scale, emergent properties of the quantum spacetime.
