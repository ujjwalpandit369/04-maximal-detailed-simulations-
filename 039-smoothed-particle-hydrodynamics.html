Interactive Simulation: Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics

Modeling Astrophysical Fluids with Lagrangian Particles

What is SPH?

The previous simulations (Cellular Automata, Lattice Boltzmann) were **Eulerian**, meaning they used a fixed grid. **Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics (SPH)** is a completely different, **Lagrangian** approach. Instead of a grid, the fluid is represented by a collection of particles. Each particle has a mass, position, and velocity, and it carries fluid properties like density and pressure.

The key idea is that these are not simple point-masses. Each particle is "smoothed" out in space by a **kernel function**. To find the density or pressure at any point, you sum up the contributions from all nearby "fuzzy" particles. This mesh-free approach is incredibly powerful for problems where a fixed grid would be useless, such as astrophysical phenomena like star formation, galaxy collisions, or supernova explosions, where the fluid is flying through empty space.

Beginner’s Guide to the Simulation

  1. The Collapsing Cloud: Click the **"Uniform Cloud"** button. You'll see a cloud of gas particles. Due to their mutual gravity (controlled by the **Gravity** slider), they will start to fall towards the center. Watch as the density (represented by color, from blue to yellow to white) increases dramatically in the center, forming high-density filaments and clumps. This is a simplified model of how stars begin to form in interstellar nebulae.
  2. The Effect of Rotation: Now, click the **"Rotating Cloud"** button. The particles now have an initial "spin." As the cloud collapses, conservation of angular momentum forces it to spin faster and flatten into a disk. A dense bar or spiral arms might form in the center. This is the fundamental reason why galaxies and solar systems are disk-shaped!
  3. Play with Physics: Try increasing the **Gas Pressure** (which resists collapse) or the **Gravity** (which drives collapse) to see how the final structure changes.

Core Theory: The SPH Formalism

In SPH, any continuous field `A(r)` is approximated by summing the values at discrete particle locations `r_j`, weighted by a smoothing kernel `W` with a characteristic smoothing length `h`:

A(r) = Σj mj * (Aj/ρj) * W(r - rj, h)

where `m_j`, `A_j`, and `ρ_j` are the mass, field value, and density of particle `j`.

Density and Pressure

The density of each particle `i` is the most fundamental calculation, found by applying the above formula to the density field itself:

ρi = Σj mj * W(ri - rj, h)

Once the density is known, the pressure `P` is typically calculated using an **equation of state**. This simulation uses a simple isothermal law: `P = cs² * ρ`, where `cs` is the sound speed.

Forces

The acceleration of each particle is determined by the sum of forces. The SPH formulation cleverly converts gradients of continuous fields into particle-based sums. The acceleration on particle `i` is:

dvi/dt = - Σj mj * [ (Pi/ρi²) + (Pj/ρj²) + Πij ] * ∇W(ri - rj, h) + Fgravity

  • The `(P/ρ²)` term is a symmetrized form of the **pressure gradient**, ensuring momentum is conserved.
  • Πij is an **artificial viscosity** term, which is crucial for handling shocks (sudden, steep changes in density and pressure) correctly. It adds a repulsive force between particles that are rapidly approaching each other.
  • Fgravity is the standard Newtonian gravitational force between particles, often "softened" at very short distances to prevent numerical blow-ups.

The particles' positions and velocities are then updated using a numerical integrator, like the Leapfrog method used here, which is well-suited for gravitational N-body problems.
