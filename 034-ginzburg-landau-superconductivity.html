Interactive Simulation: Ginzburg-Landau Theory of Superconductivity

Visualizing the Quantum Wave Function of a Superconductor

Overview for Lay Readers

Superconductivity is a quantum mechanical phenomenon on a macroscopic scale. Instead of thinking about individual electrons, we can describe all the superconducting electrons in a material with a single, unified quantum wave function, called the **order parameter** (ψ). This simulation visualizes the behavior of this wave function based on the Nobel prize-winning Ginzburg-Landau theory.

The wave function ψ has two key properties at every point in the material:

  • Amplitude (Brightness): The "strength" of the superconductivity. Bright regions are fully superconducting, while dark regions are normal (not superconducting).
  • Phase (Color): A quantum property related to the wave's rhythm. The way the phase changes from point to point determines the flow of supercurrent, which carries electricity with zero resistance.

In this simulation, you can control the temperature and magnetic field to see how they affect this quantum wave. You'll see phenomena like the **Meissner effect** (the expulsion of magnetic fields) and the formation of **vortices** (tiny whirlpools in the quantum wave where superconductivity is destroyed at the center) appear naturally from the theory.

Beginner’s Guide to the Simulation

  1. The Viewport: The main window shows a 2D slice of the superconductor.
    • Brightness: Strength of superconductivity. Bright = strong, Dark = none.
    • Color (Hue): The phase of the quantum wave.
  2. Key Controls:
    • Magnetic Field (H): Start with this at zero. The material should become uniformly bright (fully superconducting). Now, slowly increase the magnetic field.
    • G-L Parameter (κ): This number determines the *type* of superconductor.
      • Set κ < 0.7 for a **Type-I** superconductor. As you increase the field, the material will resist it until it suddenly goes dark (superconductivity is destroyed).
      • Set κ > 0.7 for a **Type-II** superconductor. As you increase the field, you'll see dark spots appear—these are **vortices**. Notice the colors swirl around each vortex, indicating a circulating supercurrent.
    • "Quench" Button: This simulates rapidly cooling the material from a normal state. You'll see a chaotic storm of vortices and anti-vortices (with opposite color swirls) that annihilate each other until a stable state is reached.

Core Theory: The Ginzburg-Landau Model

The Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory is a phenomenological theory that describes the superconducting state near the critical temperature Tc. It is based on an expansion of the free energy density `f` in terms of a complex order parameter `ψ(r)`. `|ψ(r)|²` is interpreted as the local density of superconducting electrons.

The total free energy `F` to be minimized is:

F = ∫ d³r [ α(T)|ψ|² + (β/2)|ψ|⁴ + (1/2m)|(-iħ∇ - 2eA)ψ|² + (B²/2μ₀) ]

Where `α(T)` and `β` are phenomenological parameters, `A` is the magnetic vector potential, and `B` is the magnetic field. The term `α(T) = α₀(T - T_c)` drives the phase transition.

The behavior of the system can be described by the **Time-Dependent Ginzburg-Landau (TDGL) equation**, which describes how `ψ` relaxes towards a state that minimizes the free energy:

∂ψ/∂t = -(i∇ + A)²ψ + (1 - T/T_c - |ψ|²)ψ

(in dimensionless units). This is the equation being solved in this simulation. The `(i∇ + A)²` term is the kinetic energy, and the `(1 - T/T_c - |ψ|²)ψ` term drives `|ψ|` to its equilibrium value.

Coherence Length (ξ) and Penetration Depth (λ)

The theory gives rise to two fundamental length scales:

  • Coherence Length (ξ): The characteristic length scale over which the order parameter `ψ` can vary significantly. It's essentially the minimum size of a superconducting-normal boundary.
  • Penetration Depth (λ): The characteristic length scale over which an external magnetic field is screened and decays inside the superconductor.

The Ginzburg-Landau Parameter (κ)

The ratio of these two lengths, κ = λ/ξ, is a crucial dimensionless constant that determines the type of superconductor:

  • If κ < 1/√2: **Type-I Superconductor**. The coherence length is large. It is energetically unfavorable for interfaces to form, so the material exhibits a complete Meissner effect up to a critical field Hc, at which point the entire material becomes normal.
  • If κ > 1/√2: **Type-II Superconductor**. The penetration depth is large. It becomes energetically favorable for the magnetic field to penetrate the material by creating an array of vortices (or flux tubes) above a lower critical field Hc1. Each vortex has a normal core of size `~ξ` and is surrounded by screening currents over a distance `~λ`.
