Interactive Simulation: Quantum Transport with the NEGF Formalism

This simulation introduces the Non-Equilibrium Green's Function (NEGF) formalism, a powerful theoretical framework for modeling quantum transport—the flow of electrons—through nanoscale devices. As transistors and other electronic components shrink to the atomic scale, quantum effects like tunneling and interference become dominant. NEGF provides a rigorous way to calculate properties like the current-voltage (I-V) curve and transmission probability for these quantum systems.

The NEGF Approach: Leads and a Central Device

The core idea of NEGF is to partition the system into three parts: a "left lead," a "right lead," and a central "device region" where the interesting quantum scattering occurs. The leads are assumed to be large, ideal conductors that act as reservoirs of electrons, each in its own thermal equilibrium. A voltage bias applied between the leads drives the system out of equilibrium, causing a net flow of current through the central device.

Instead of working with wavefunctions, NEGF uses a more powerful mathematical object called a Green's function, G. The Green's function contains all the information about the quantum states and their propagation within the device. A key concept is the self-energy, Σ (Sigma), which describes how the semi-infinite leads connect to and interact with the finite central device region. By solving a set of equations relating the Green's function and the self-energies, one can calculate the key physical observables.

Transmission and the Landauer Formula

The most important output of an NEGF calculation is the transmission coefficient, T(E). This function, which depends on energy E, gives the probability that an electron injected from the left lead with energy E will successfully travel through the device and emerge in the right lead. T(E) will typically show sharp peaks at energies that correspond to the resonant quantum states (energy levels) of the central device region. An electron whose energy matches one of these resonant levels can tunnel through the device easily (T(E) ≈ 1), while an electron with an off-resonant energy will be reflected (T(E) ≈ 0).

Once T(E) is known, the total current flowing through the device for a given voltage V is calculated using the Landauer formula:

I(V) = (2e²/h) ∫ [fL(E) - fR(E)] T(E) dE
where fL and fR are the Fermi-Dirac distributions of the left and right leads, which are offset by the applied voltage. This integral essentially counts the number of available states in one lead that can transmit to available empty states in the other lead, weighted by the transmission probability.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. What is a Green's function?

In physics, a Green's function is the response of a system to an impulsive, point-like source. It essentially tells you how a disturbance propagates through the system. In quantum mechanics, the Green's function G(E) contains complete information about the system's energy levels and how particles propagate at a given energy E.

2. Why is it called "Non-Equilibrium"?

Because the NEGF formalism is specifically designed to handle systems where a current is flowing, which is by definition a non-equilibrium state. The left and right leads are held at different chemical potentials (due to the applied voltage), so there is a net flow of particles, and the system is not in thermal equilibrium.

3. What is the "self-energy" (Σ)?

The self-energy is a crucial concept in NEGF. It is a mathematical term that describes the effect of connecting the semi-infinite leads to the finite central device region. It effectively modifies the Hamiltonian of the device to account for the fact that electrons can now enter and leave, giving the device's energy levels a finite lifetime and a broadening.

4. What is the Transmission Coefficient T(E)?

T(E) is the probability that an electron injected at one end of the device with energy E will successfully make it through to the other end. For a simple device with discrete energy levels (like a quantum dot), T(E) will show sharp peaks at those energy levels. These are called "resonant tunneling" peaks.

5. What is the Landauer Formula?

The Landauer formula is a fundamental result that relates a macroscopic quantity, the electrical current (I), to a quantum mechanical property, the transmission coefficient (T(E)). It states that the current is proportional to the integral of T(E) over the energy window between the chemical potentials of the two leads. It beautifully connects the microscopic quantum world to measurable device properties.

6. How does the main visualization work?

It shows a schematic of a "device," which in this case is a 1D potential profile with two barriers. The simulation calculates the Local Density of States (LDOS) on a grid within this device. The LDOS shows where quantum states are allowed to exist. You will see high LDOS values at the resonant energy levels inside the quantum well between the barriers.

7. What is the "Local Density of States" (LDOS)?

The LDOS at a particular point in space and energy, LDOS(x, E), tells you how many available quantum states there are at that specific location and energy. It is directly related to the imaginary part of the Green's function. In the simulation, you see it integrated over one dimension to give LDOS(x).

8. Why does the I-V curve show steps?

This is a characteristic feature of quantum transport through a device with discrete energy levels, sometimes called the "Coulomb staircase." As you increase the voltage, the energy window for conduction widens. The current stays flat until the window expands enough to include a new transmission peak (a resonant level). When it does, the current jumps up to a new plateau. Each step corresponds to a new quantum level becoming available for transport.

9. How does the "Barrier Height" slider affect the simulation?

Increasing the barrier height makes it harder for electrons to tunnel from the leads into the central region and out again. This makes the coupling between the leads and the device weaker. As a result, the resonant energy levels become sharper and narrower, and the transmission peaks in T(E) will become much sharper but also smaller in magnitude.

10. What is "ballistic transport"?

Ballistic transport is when electrons travel through a conductor without scattering. This happens in very clean, small conductors where the electron's mean free path is longer than the device length. The NEGF formalism is well-suited to describing ballistic transport.

11. How is this different from diffusive transport (Ohm's Law)?

Diffusive transport, described by Ohm's law, occurs in large, macroscopic conductors where electrons scatter many times from impurities and vibrations. In this regime, the current is simply proportional to the voltage (I = V/R). In the quantum (ballistic) regime, the I-V curve is generally non-linear and exhibits quantum effects like the steps seen in the simulation.

12. What is a "quantum dot"?

A quantum dot is a tiny semiconductor nanocrystal that is so small that its electrons are confined in all three dimensions. This means it has discrete, atom-like energy levels. A single quantum dot placed between two leads is a classic example of a system that can be modeled with NEGF and exhibits resonant tunneling.

13. How is the NEGF calculation actually performed?

The device's Hamiltonian is represented as a matrix. The self-energies of the leads are also calculated as matrices. The core of the calculation involves inverting a large matrix, [ (E+iη)I - H - ΣL - ΣR ], to find the Green's function G. From G and the self-energies, the transmission T(E) can be calculated. This is computationally intensive.

14. What are the different Green's functions (retarded, advanced, lesser)?

These are different types of Green's functions used in the full NEGF formalism. The retarded Green's function GR describes the propagation of particles and the available states (LDOS). The "lesser" Green's function G< describes the occupation of those states. Both are needed to calculate the current in a non-equilibrium system.

15. Can NEGF include electron-electron interactions?

Yes, but it is very difficult. In the simple case, NEGF assumes non-interacting electrons. Including interactions (like in DFT) requires calculating a more complex self-energy term, often within a self-consistent loop. This is the basis of the powerful NEGF-DFT method.

16. What is "Coulomb Blockade"?

In a very small quantum dot, the energy required to add a single extra electron (the "charging energy") can be very large. If the applied voltage is not high enough to overcome this charging energy, no current can flow. The current is "blockaded." As you increase the voltage, the current turns on in sharp steps, corresponding to allowing 0, 1, 2, ... electrons onto the dot. The steps in the simulated I-V curve are an analogue of this effect.

17. What does the NEGF Formalism Diagram show?

It's a schematic flowchart. It illustrates that the central object is the Green's function G, which is calculated from the Hamiltonian H and the lead self-energies Σ. From G, one can then compute all the key observables, like the transmission T(E), the density of states, and the current I.

18. What is a "single-molecule transistor"?

This is a device where the "central region" is a single organic molecule chemically bonded to two metal leads. It is the ultimate limit of miniaturization. NEGF-DFT is the primary tool used to predict and understand the electrical conductance of such devices.

19. What is the role of the imaginary part 'η' in the Green's function?

The small imaginary part, iη, is a mathematical tool used in the definition of the retarded Green's function. Physically, it corresponds to an infinitesimal broadening of the energy levels, which is necessary to make the calculations well-behaved.

20. Why is the transmission T(E) a probability?

It represents the quantum mechanical probability for a single electron wave to tunnel through the potential barriers of the device. According to the rules of quantum mechanics, this probability can range from 0 (perfect reflection) to 1 (perfect transmission).

21. How does this relate to scanning tunneling microscopy (STM)?

STM works on a similar principle. The STM tip acts as one "lead," the sample surface acts as the other "lead," and the vacuum gap between them is the "device." The tunneling current that flows is extremely sensitive to the distance and the Local Density of States of the surface, which is what allows STM to image individual atoms.
