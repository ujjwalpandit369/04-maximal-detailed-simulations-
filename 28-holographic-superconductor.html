Beginner's Guide: Black Holes with "Hair"

A famous idea in black hole physics is the "no-hair theorem," which states that a black hole is incredibly simple, described only by its mass, charge, and spin. Any other complexity (the "hair") is swallowed by the horizon. However, in the strange world of Anti-de Sitter (AdS) space, this isn't always true. Under the right conditions, a black hole can spontaneously grow "hair" made of a scalar field, which is a type of energy field that can exist in a "condensed" state.

This simulation shows this amazing process, which is the holographic dual of a superconductor phase transition. The user controls the temperature of a charged black hole in AdS. Above a critical temperature `T_c`, the black hole is "bald"—the scalar field `Ψ` is zero everywhere outside the horizon, as shown in the left panel. But when you lower the temperature below `T_c`, the system becomes unstable. It is now energetically favorable for a cloud of scalar field to condense around the black hole, giving it "hair." You will see a non-zero profile for `Ψ(r)` appear. The plot on the right shows the strength of this "hair" (the condensate) as a function of temperature. You can see that the condensate only appears below `T_c`, a classic signature of a second-order phase transition, just like in a real-world superconductor.

Core Theory: The Superconducting Instability

The holographic superconductor is a model that realizes a superconducting phase transition in a boundary CFT via a classical gravitational instability in the bulk. The setup consists of a charged black hole (AdS-Reissner-Nordström) and a charged scalar field `Ψ`.

The equation of motion for the scalar field `Ψ` in the background of the black hole is a complex second-order ODE. The key insight is that the effective mass of the scalar field receives a contribution from the electric field of the black hole. Near the horizon of a charged black hole, this can make the effective mass-squared *negative*. In flat space this would be a catastrophic instability (a tachyon). However, because of the confining walls of AdS space, a negative mass-squared near the horizon does not lead to a runaway instability, but instead allows for stable, normalizable "bound states" of the scalar field to exist. These are the "hair."

This instability only occurs when the black hole's temperature `T` is low enough. The temperature of a charged black hole depends on its mass and charge. For a given charge, there is a critical temperature `T_c` below which the instability is triggered.

  • For `T > T_c`, the only regular solution to the field equations is `Ψ(r) = 0`. The black hole is bald.
  • For `T < T_c`, a new, non-trivial solution with `Ψ(r) ≠ 0` appears. The black hole spontaneously grows scalar hair.
This simulation solves the ODE for `Ψ(r)` using a shooting method. We want a solution that is regular at the horizon and vanishes at the AdS boundary (`r -> ∞`). We can start integrating from just outside the horizon, but we need to know the initial value and its derivative. By analyzing the equations at the horizon, we can find the derivative in terms of the field value. We are then left with one free parameter to "shoot" for: the value of the field at the horizon, `Ψ(r_+)`. The algorithm adjusts this value until the solution satisfies the boundary condition at infinity. The value of `Ψ(r_+)` is proportional to the expectation value of the superconducting condensate operator `⟨O⟩` in the boundary theory. The plot of `⟨O⟩` vs. `T` shows the characteristic behavior of a second-order phase transition.
