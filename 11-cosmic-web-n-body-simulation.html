Overview

This simulation visualizes the fundamental process that sculpts the universe on its largest scales: the relentless pull of gravity. Starting from a nearly uniform distribution of matter in an expanding universe, this N-body simulation shows how tiny, random quantum fluctuations in the early universe are amplified by gravity over billions of years. These initial seeds grow into a vast, intricate network of filaments, clusters, and voids known as the "cosmic web." You are watching a 2D slice of a simulated universe, where each point of light represents a large clump of dark matter. By adjusting cosmological parameters like the density of matter (Ω_m) and dark energy (Ω_Λ), you can see how the very fabric of this cosmic structure is determined by the underlying laws and composition of the universe. This simulation uses a Particle-Mesh (PM) algorithm to efficiently calculate gravitational forces, allowing us to watch billions of years of cosmic evolution unfold in seconds.

Full Exposition

Beginner's Guide: The Universe's Gravitational Recipe

Imagine a perfectly smooth, evenly spread-out fog. This is like the early universe: incredibly uniform and boring. Now, imagine that some parts of the fog are, just by random chance, infinitesimally denser than other parts. What happens over time? Gravity, the ultimate cosmic architect, gets to work. A region that is slightly denser has slightly more gravity. It begins to pull in matter from its surroundings. As it does, it becomes even denser and its gravitational pull becomes even stronger. This is a runaway process called gravitational instability. The rich get richer.

Over billions of years, this simple process transforms the smooth, boring universe into the complex and beautiful one we see today. The slightly over-dense regions grow into massive clusters of galaxies, the most gravitationally bound objects in the cosmos. The slightly under-dense regions have their matter stripped away, expanding to become vast, empty cosmic voids. The material in between is stretched into long, tenuous bridges and walls, forming the filaments of the cosmic web. This simulation shows this entire process. We start with thousands of "particles" (each representing a vast amount of dark matter) spread almost uniformly. As time progresses (tracked by the scale factor 'a'), you can see them clustering together, evacuating voids, and forming the iconic web-like pattern. It's a direct visualization of how gravity, acting on tiny initial imperfections, built the grand structure of our universe.

Core Theory: N-body Simulations in an Expanding Universe

This simulation solves the equations of motion for N particles representing dark matter in a universe governed by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric. The key challenge is to model the interplay between the global expansion of the universe and the local gravitational attraction of matter.

1. Comoving Coordinates: We work in "comoving coordinates" (`x`). This is like drawing the simulation on a rubber sheet that is being stretched. The physical distance `r` between two points is `r = a(t) * x`, where `a(t)` is the cosmic scale factor that describes the expansion of the universe. In these coordinates, a particle that is only affected by the cosmic expansion remains at a fixed position `x`.

2. Equations of Motion: The motion of a particle's peculiar velocity `v = a(t) * dx/dt` is described by:
d²x/dt² + 2H(t) dx/dt = - (1/a²) ∇Φ_c
where `H(t) = (da/dt)/a` is the Hubble parameter, `Φ_c` is the gravitational potential calculated from the comoving density of matter, and the `2H(t) dx/dt` term is the "Hubble drag," a friction-like term that damps peculiar velocities as the universe expands.

3. The Particle-Mesh (PM) Method: Calculating the gravitational force `∇Φ_c` directly by summing up all pairs of particles is an O(N²) problem, which is computationally prohibitive. The PM method offers an efficient alternative:

  • Density Assignment: The mass of the N particles is assigned to a regular grid, creating a density field `ρ(x)`. This simulation uses the simple Nearest-Grid-Point (NGP) scheme.
  • Potential Calculation: The gravitational potential is related to the density by the Poisson equation: `∇²Φ_c = 4πGρ_c`. This equation, which is a differential equation in real space, becomes a simple algebraic equation in Fourier space: `-k² Φ_k = 4πG ρ_k`. We use a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to transform `ρ` to `ρ_k`, solve for `Φ_k`, and then use an inverse FFT to transform back to `Φ` on the grid. This is extremely fast, scaling as O(M log M) where M is the number of grid cells.
  • Force Calculation: The gravitational force on the grid is found by taking the gradient of the potential, `F = -∇Φ`, using finite differences.
  • Force Interpolation: The force is interpolated from the grid points back to each particle's actual position, allowing us to update its velocity.

4. Time Integration: We use a Leapfrog (or Kick-Drift-Kick) integrator. It is a symplectic integrator that conserves energy well for long-term gravitational simulations. The steps for a time-step `Δt` are:

  1. Kick: Update velocities by a half-step using the current forces.
  2. Drift: Update positions by a full time-step using the new velocities.
  3. Re-calculate forces at the new positions.
  4. Kick: Update velocities by another half-step using the new forces.
This process is repeated, and at each step, the cosmic scale factor `a(t)` is also updated according to the Friedmann equation: `H(a)² = H₀² (Ω_m/a³ + Ω_Λ)`.

Q&A

1. What do the particles in the simulation represent?

They represent dark matter. In our universe, about 85% of the matter is "dark matter," which does not interact with light but does interact with gravity. On large scales, the gravity from dark matter is the dominant force shaping the cosmic web. The galaxies and gas that we can see (baryonic matter) make up only a small fraction of the mass and tend to fall into the deepest gravitational wells created by the dark matter. So, by simulating only dark matter, we can get a very accurate picture of the large-scale structure of the universe. Each "particle" in the simulation is not a single dark matter particle, but represents a huge collection of them, typically with a mass of billions of solar masses.

2. What is the difference between Ω_m and Ω_Λ?

Ω (Omega) is the density parameter, which measures the density of a component relative to the critical density needed to make the universe spatially flat. Ω_m (Omega Matter) represents the density of all matter, including dark matter and baryonic matter. Matter's gravitational pull is attractive and causes the expansion of the universe to slow down. Ω_Λ (Omega Lambda) represents the density of "dark energy," a mysterious component that has a constant energy density as the universe expands. It acts as a form of cosmic repulsion, causing the expansion of the universe to accelerate. In our standard model of cosmology (ΛCDM), we live in a universe with Ω_m ≈ 0.3 and Ω_Λ ≈ 0.7. You can see the effect of dark energy by setting Ω_Λ to a high value; structure formation will slow down and stop at late times as the cosmic acceleration overwhelms gravity.

3. Why does the simulation use "comoving coordinates"?

Working in comoving coordinates is a mathematical convenience that is essential for cosmological simulations. It allows us to separate the global expansion of the universe from the local motion of particles due to gravity. The grid in the simulation is a comoving grid; you can think of it as being painted on the expanding fabric of spacetime. If there were no gravity, all the particles would stay at fixed coordinates on this grid. The motion you see in the simulation is the "peculiar motion"—the extra movement of particles as they fall into over-dense regions. The simulation calculates this peculiar motion and then adds the effect of cosmic expansion back in when determining the particles' true physical velocities and the strength of gravity. This makes the problem computationally tractable.
