Beginner's Guide: Designing a Cosmic Cycle

The previous simulations have shown different cosmological scenarios—a single "ekpyrotic" collision, a "cyclic" universe of endless bounces, and a slow "brane inflation." What determines which of these scenarios actually happens? The answer is the "potential energy" `V(y)` between the branes, where `y` is their separation in the extra dimension. The shape of this potential dictates the force between the branes and governs their motion. This simulation is a laboratory that lets you be the architect of a universe by designing this potential.

The plot on the left shows the potential energy `V` as a function of the brane separation `y`. The yellow ball represents the state of the two-brane system. When you click "Release Brane," the ball will start to "roll" on this potential landscape. The plot on the right shows the resulting trajectory, `y(t)`, over time. You can use the sliders to change the strength of the long-range attraction (B) and the short-range repulsion (A). Try creating a potential that leads to a single bounce (like the ekpyrotic model) or one that traps the ball, leading to an endless cycle of collisions and rebounds (the cyclic model). This lab shows how the entire history and fate of the universe, in these models, can be determined by the fundamental shape of the inter-brane potential.

Core Theory: The Radion Potential and Moduli Stabilization

In string theory and M-theory, the properties of the extra dimensions of space—their size, shape, and the distances between objects like branes—are described by scalar fields called "moduli fields." In the context of brane cosmology, the field `φ` that describes the distance `y` between our brane and another is called the "radion." The dynamics of the branes are governed by the potential energy of this radion field, `V(φ)`.

The precise form of this potential is a subject of intense research and depends on the details of the underlying string theory compactification. However, different cosmological scenarios correspond to different general shapes of `V(φ)`:

  • Ekpyrotic/Cyclic Models: These require a potential that is attractive at large distances but becomes sharply repulsive at short distances, allowing for a "bounce." This simulation models this with a Lennard-Jones-like potential: `V(y) = A/y^n - B/y^m` (with n > m). The `A/y^n` term provides the repulsion, and the `-B/y^m` term provides the attraction.
  • Brane Inflation: These models require a potential that is very flat over a long range. For example, the attraction between a D-brane and an anti-D-brane leads to a simple Coulomb-like potential `V(y) = C - D/y^p`. If the branes are far apart, the potential is very flat, the force is very weak, and the branes approach each other very slowly. This "slow-roll" motion drives cosmic inflation on the branes. The collision at the end corresponds to the branes annihilating and reheating the universe.
This simulation solves for the classical motion of the brane system in the user-defined potential using a Verlet integrator, a numerical method well-suited for conserving energy in mechanical systems. By exploring how the trajectory `y(t)` changes as you vary the parameters of `V(y)`, you are exploring the landscape of possible string theory cosmologies.
