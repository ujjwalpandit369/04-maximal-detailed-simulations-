Interactive Simulation: Protostellar Disk Fragmentation & Binary Formation

This simulation explores the physics of protostellar disks—the rotating disks of gas and dust that are the birthplaces of planets and binary star systems. We will focus on the process of gravitational instability, where a disk can become unstable under its own gravity and fragment into dense clumps. This fragmentation is a leading theory for the formation of massive gas giant planets and companion stars.

The Toomre Q Parameter and Disk Stability

As a rotating molecular cloud core collapses, the conservation of angular momentum forces the material into a flattened, rotating disk around the central protostar. The fate of this disk is governed by a competition between several forces. Its own self-gravity tries to make it collapse into clumps. This is counteracted by thermal pressure (which resists compression) and rotational shear (which tries to rip clumps apart). The balance between these effects is captured by a single dimensionless number: the Toomre Q parameter.

The parameter is defined as Q = (csκ)/(πGΣ), where cs is the sound speed (related to temperature), κ is the epicyclic frequency (related to rotational shear), and Σ is the surface density of the disk. A high Q value means the disk is hot and/or has strong shear, making it stable against collapse. A low Q value means the disk is massive (high Σ) and/or cold, making it susceptible to its own gravity. The critical value is Q=1. If Q < 1, the disk is gravitationally unstable and will likely form spiral arms that can then fragment into self-gravitating clumps.

Fragmentation: A Path to Planets and Stars

When a disk becomes unstable and fragments, the resulting clumps can have several fates. If a clump is massive enough, it can continue to contract and will eventually form a companion star, resulting in a binary or multiple star system. In fact, most stars in the galaxy are in binary systems, and disk fragmentation is a likely formation channel. If the clumps are less massive, they may form gas giant planets. This "gravitational instability" model of planet formation is an alternative to the "core accretion" model (where a rocky core forms first and then accretes gas) and may be particularly important for forming massive planets at large distances from their host star.

The stability of a disk depends on its ability to cool. If the disk can cool efficiently, it can maintain a low Q value and continue to fragment. If it cannot cool fast enough, the energy from gravitational collapse will heat up the disk, raising its pressure and Q value, thus stabilizing it against further fragmentation. This simulation allows you to explore these concepts by changing the properties of the disk and observing the onset of instability.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. Why do protostars have disks?

It's due to the conservation of angular momentum. The initial molecular cloud core is always rotating slightly. As the core collapses under gravity, its rotation speed increases, just like a spinning ice skater pulling in their arms. This rotation prevents gas from falling directly onto the protostar, forcing it into a flattened, rotating structure—the protostellar disk.

2. What is the Toomre Q parameter?

It is a dimensionless number that measures the local stability of a rotating, self-gravitating disk. It represents the ratio of stabilizing forces (thermal pressure and rotational shear) to the destabilizing force of gravity. If Q > 1, the disk is stable. If Q < 1, the disk is unstable to the growth of perturbations and is likely to form spiral arms and fragment into clumps.

3. What is the difference between gravitational instability and core accretion for planet formation?

They are two competing theories. Core accretion is the "bottom-up" model: small dust grains stick together to form planetesimals, which grow into rocky cores, and a large enough core can then rapidly accrete a massive gas envelope. Gravitational instability is the "top-down" model: a massive gas disk fragments directly into giant, self-gravitating clumps that contract to form gas giant planets. Gravitational instability may be better at forming massive planets far from their star.

4. How does the disk mass slider affect stability?

Increasing the disk mass increases its surface density (Σ). Since Q is inversely proportional to Σ (Q ∝ 1/Σ), a more massive disk has a lower Q value and is therefore less stable and more likely to fragment. In the simulation, this will cause spiral arms and clumps to appear more readily.

5. How does the disk temperature slider affect stability?

Increasing the disk temperature increases the gas pressure and the sound speed (cs). Since Q is directly proportional to the sound speed (Q ∝ cs), a hotter disk has a higher Q value and is more stable. In the simulation, a higher temperature will tend to smooth out the disk and prevent fragmentation.

6. What are the spiral arms in the disk?

When a disk is close to being gravitationally unstable (Q is low but still > 1), it can support spiral density waves. These are rotating, spiral patterns of higher density gas. These waves are very important because they are an efficient way to transport angular momentum outwards, allowing mass to flow inwards onto the star.

7. What is the "angular momentum problem" in star formation?

The initial rotating core has far too much angular momentum to collapse directly into a star-sized object. The vast majority of this angular momentum must be removed or redistributed. Protostellar disks are the solution: they allow the angular momentum to be transported outwards (e.g., by spiral waves or turbulence), enabling the mass to accrete onto the central protostar.

8. How are binary stars formed?

Disk fragmentation is a leading theory. If the disk fragments into two or more massive clumps, these clumps can contract into protostars and eventually settle into a stable orbit around each other, forming a binary or multiple-star system. The fact that most massive stars have companions suggests this is a common process.

9. How does the main disk simulation work?

It's a simplified 2D fluid simulation on a grid. It solves equations for a rotating fluid under the influence of gravity (from the central star and the disk itself) and pressure. The user controls parameters that feed into the Toomre Q calculation, and the simulation shows the resulting behavior—a smooth disk for Q>1 and a fragmenting one for Q<1.

10. What is "epicyclic frequency" (κ)?

It is the frequency at which a particle in a disk oscillates radially when it is slightly perturbed from its circular orbit. It is a measure of the rotational shear and the stability of orbits. In a Keplerian disk (orbiting a central point mass), κ is equal to the orbital frequency Ω.

11. Why is disk cooling important for fragmentation?

For a clump to collapse, it must be able to radiate away the heat generated by its gravitational contraction. If it cannot cool efficiently, the internal pressure will build up, increasing its Q value and halting the collapse. Therefore, fragmentation is favored in regions of the disk that can cool quickly.

12. What does the Toomre Q plot show?

It shows the calculated value of Q as a function of radius from the central star. It will typically be high in the hot inner regions of the disk and high in the low-density outer regions. There is often a "sweet spot" in the middle of the disk where the density is high and the temperature is low, causing Q to dip below the critical value of 1, which is where fragmentation will occur.

13. How do we observe these disks and fragments?

Telescopes like ALMA (the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array) are revolutionary for this field. They can observe the thermal emission from the cold dust and gas in these disks at very high resolution, allowing astronomers to see spiral arms, gaps carved by planets, and in some cases, the dense clumps that may be forming planets or companion stars.

14. Do all stars have disks?

It is believed that nearly all young stars form with a protostellar disk. However, these disks are dispersed over time, typically within a few million years, by processes like photoevaporation from the central star and the formation of planets.

15. What is the "snow line"?

The snow line (or ice line) is the radius in a protostellar disk beyond which it is cold enough for volatile compounds like water, ammonia, and methane to condense into solid ice grains. This dramatically increases the amount of solid material available to build planets, which is why gas and ice giants are found in the outer parts of solar systems.

16. How does the Angular Momentum Transport diagram work?

It's a cartoon showing a spiral arm passing through the disk. As gas enters the dense arm, it is shocked and slowed down, losing angular momentum. As it leaves the arm, it speeds up, gaining angular momentum. The net effect is that the spiral arm torques the inner disk material, forcing it inwards, while torquing the outer disk material outwards, thus transporting angular momentum.

17. What is the "viscosity" of a protostellar disk?

The gas in the disk is not perfectly smooth. Turbulence and magnetic fields can create an "effective viscosity" that causes friction between adjacent rotating layers. This viscosity also transports angular momentum outwards and allows for accretion, and is a key parameter in disk models.

18. How long do protostellar disks last?

Typically, they last for about 3 to 10 million years. After this time, the gas has either been accreted onto the star, incorporated into planets, or dispersed by stellar winds and radiation.

19. What is a "T Tauri" star?

A T Tauri star is a type of young, pre-main-sequence star (less than about 10 million years old) that is still surrounded by a protostellar disk and is in the process of contracting onto the main sequence. They are often magnetically active and have strong stellar winds.

20. Why are most stars found in binary or multiple systems?

This is a major question in star formation. The fact that most stars (especially massive ones) have companions suggests that fragmentation of disks and cores is a very common and efficient process. Studying these young systems helps us understand the origin of the stars we see today.

21. What happens to the fragments after they form?

They continue to contract under their own gravity. They also interact with the disk, which can cause them to migrate inwards towards the star. This migration can explain the existence of "hot Jupiters"—gas giant planets found very close to their host stars.
