Interactive Simulation: Turbulent Star Formation & Core Collapse

This simulation explores the modern paradigm of star formation, where interstellar turbulence plays a pivotal, dual role. Far from being just a disruptive force, turbulence in Giant Molecular Clouds (GMCs) is now understood to be the primary mechanism that creates the dense structures—filaments and cores—that subsequently collapse under their own gravity to form stars. This process is known as turbulent fragmentation.

The Role of Supersonic Turbulence

Stars are born in the coldest, densest regions of the interstellar medium, known as Giant Molecular Clouds. These clouds are not smooth and uniform; instead, they are characterized by chaotic, supersonic turbulent motions. This turbulence is powerful enough to support the cloud as a whole against its own gravity, preventing it from collapsing into a single, gigantic star. However, the supersonic nature of the turbulence creates a web of interacting shock waves within the cloud. These shocks compress the gas, sweeping it up into a complex network of dense filaments. Where these filaments intersect, they can form even denser, gravitationally unstable clumps known as prestellar cores.

This is the dual role of turbulence: on large scales, it provides support, while on small scales, it creates the very over-densities that are the seeds of new stars. This process naturally explains why stars form in clusters and groups, rather than uniformly throughout a cloud.

Gravitational Collapse and the Jeans Instability

Once a dense core is formed, its fate is determined by a competition between its internal thermal pressure (which tries to make it expand) and its self-gravity (which tries to make it collapse). The critical condition for collapse is known as the Jeans Instability. A core is gravitationally unstable and will collapse if its mass is greater than the "Jeans mass," a value which depends on the core's temperature and density. Since the cores formed by turbulence have a range of masses, only the most massive ones will be immediately unstable and begin to collapse. The distribution of these core masses (the Core Mass Function, or CMF) looks remarkably similar to the distribution of stellar masses (the Initial Mass Function, or IMF), suggesting that the mass of a star is largely set by the properties of its parent turbulent core.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

1. What is turbulence?

Turbulence is a state of fluid flow characterized by chaotic, stochastic property changes. It includes a wide range of swirling structures called "eddies." In GMCs, this turbulence is "supersonic," meaning the chaotic gas motions are faster than the speed of sound in the cloud, which creates shock waves.

2. Where does the turbulence in Giant Molecular Clouds (GMCs) come from?

The exact source is a topic of debate, but it's likely a combination of factors. Energy injected by supernova explosions from previous generations of stars, gravitational instabilities within the galaxy, and the combined outflows from other young stars all contribute to stirring up the gas in a GMC.

3. What is the Jeans Instability / Jeans Mass?

The Jeans Instability is the condition for the gravitational collapse of a gas cloud. A cloud is held up by its internal thermal pressure. If its self-gravity is strong enough to overwhelm this pressure, it will collapse. The Jeans Mass is the minimum mass required for a cloud of a given size and temperature to become unstable and collapse.

4. How does turbulence create dense cores?

Supersonic turbulence creates a network of shock waves. As gas passes through these shocks, it is compressed to higher densities. The interaction of multiple shocks creates a web-like structure of dense filaments, and the densest regions of all—prestellar cores—are typically found at the intersections of these filaments.

5. What is the Core Mass Function (CMF)?

The CMF is the distribution of masses of the prestellar cores found within a molecular cloud. It shows how many cores of a given mass exist. Observationally, the CMF looks very similar in shape to the stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF), with many more low-mass cores than high-mass cores.

6. What is the Initial Mass Function (IMF)?

The IMF describes the distribution of initial masses for a population of stars. It tells us that low-mass stars (like the Sun or smaller) are very common, while very massive stars are extremely rare. The similarity between the CMF and IMF strongly suggests that the final mass of a star is primarily determined by the mass of its parent core.

7. What is the "dual role" of turbulence?

On the large scale of an entire GMC, the kinetic energy of the turbulence provides a source of pressure that supports the cloud against overall gravitational collapse. However, on small scales, the same turbulence creates dense cores that are themselves gravitationally unstable. So, turbulence both prevents and enables star formation simultaneously.

8. How does the main simulation work?

It's a simplified 2D fluid simulation. It starts with a uniform density field. A pre-calculated turbulent velocity field is used to "advect" the density, moving it around the grid. This process naturally causes the density to pile up in certain regions, forming the filamentary structures and cores seen in the visualization.

9. What does the "Turbulent Mach Number" control?

The Mach number is the ratio of the turbulent velocity to the sound speed. A higher Mach number means the turbulence is more supersonic. In the simulation, a higher Mach number leads to stronger shocks and therefore higher density contrasts, creating more defined and denser filaments and cores.

10. What is a "filament" in star formation?

Observations (e.g., with the Herschel Space Observatory) have shown that molecular clouds are not blob-like, but are dominated by a network of long, thin filamentary structures. Most prestellar cores and young stars are found to be located within these dense filaments.

11. How does the Jeans Instability diagram work?

It shows a cartoon gas cloud with arrows representing the inward pull of gravity and the outward push of pressure. The "Jeans Mass" slider sets the critical mass for collapse. When the cloud's mass (set by another slider, conceptually) is greater than the Jeans mass, the gravity arrows grow larger and an "Unstable" indicator lights up.

12. What happens during core collapse?

Once a core becomes gravitationally unstable, it begins to collapse from the inside out. The central region becomes extremely dense, forming a "protostar." The protostar continues to grow by accreting material from the surrounding collapsing envelope, often through an accretion disk. This process is animated in the "Single Core Collapse" visualization.

13. Why is star formation so inefficient?

In a typical GMC, only a few percent of the total gas mass is converted into stars over the cloud's lifetime. This is believed to be because of the regulating effects of turbulence and magnetic fields (which provide support) and feedback from the first stars that form (which can disperse the cloud).

14. How long does this turbulent fragmentation process take?

The turbulent motions can shape the cloud and form cores over a few million years, which is the typical "dynamical time" of a GMC. The subsequent collapse of an individual core to form a star is a much faster process, taking a few hundred thousand years.

15. Does gravity influence the turbulence?

Yes. As the cloud contracts under its own gravity, this can drive further turbulent motions. This "gravitational driving" can help sustain the turbulence against dissipation, but this is a complex non-linear process not included in the simplified simulation here.

16. What is a "protostar"?

A protostar is the hot, dense central object formed from the collapse of a prestellar core. It is not yet a true star because its core is not hot enough for nuclear fusion. Its luminosity comes from the heat generated by its gravitational contraction and the accretion of new material.

17. How does the CMF chart work?

After the main simulation runs for a while, it identifies the dense cores. It calculates the mass of each core (proportional to its integrated density) and then creates a histogram of these masses. The resulting chart shows the number of cores found in different mass bins, illustrating the CMF.

18. What is "competitive accretion"?

This is an alternative model for setting stellar masses. It suggests that all protostars start small, but they exist in a clustered environment and compete for gas from a common reservoir. The protostars that happen to be in the center of the cluster's gravitational potential well can accrete more material and grow to be massive stars. This is in contrast to the turbulent fragmentation model, where the star's mass is largely pre-determined by its parent core's mass.

19. What role do magnetic fields play?

Magnetic fields are also a crucial ingredient. They can provide additional support against gravitational collapse, and they can channel gas flows, helping to shape the filamentary structures. They are the subject of the final simulation in this series.

20. How do we observe these cores and filaments?

They are observed using telescopes that can detect the faint thermal emission from cold dust grains at sub-millimeter and millimeter wavelengths (like the ALMA telescope) or by observing the emission from tracer molecules (like CO) at radio wavelengths.

21. Is the simulation 2D or 3D?

This simulation is in 2D to make it runnable in a browser. Real turbulence and gravitational collapse are, of course, 3D processes. However, a 2D simulation can still capture the essential physics of turbulent compression and fragmentation.
