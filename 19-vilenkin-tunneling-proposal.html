Beginner's Guide: Two Recipes for a Universe

In the last simulation, we explored the Hartle-Hawking "No-Boundary" idea, where the universe emerges from a timeless, edge-less, 4D Euclidean space, much like the North Pole on a globe. This simulation introduces a competing idea from the physicist Alexander Vilenkin, known as the "Tunneling Proposal." Vilenkin's idea is perhaps more intuitive: the universe arises by quantum tunneling from literally "nothing"—a state of zero size and zero energy. The boundary condition is not that there is no boundary, but that at the boundary (at size `a=0`), the wave function must correspond to a universe that is expanding. It's a cosmic birth, not just a smooth transition.

This seemingly small difference in starting assumptions leads to a dramatically different prediction. You can use the dropdown menu to switch between the two models and see for yourself. The Hartle-Hawking model (select from the menu) predicts that the universe is most likely to be born onto a high-energy "plateau" of a potential, which is perfect for starting cosmic inflation. The Vilenkin model, however, predicts the opposite. Its wave function `Ψ` is proportional to `exp(-1/3V)`, where `V` is the potential energy. This means it strongly prefers the universe to be born at a state of *low* potential energy. This makes starting inflation in the Vilenkin model a much less probable event, a key point of contention between these two beautiful theories of our ultimate origin.

Core Theory: Comparing Boundary Conditions

The core difference between the Hartle-Hawking (HH) and Vilenkin (V) proposals lies in the boundary conditions they impose on the solutions to the Wheeler-DeWitt equation. This, in turn, changes the resulting wave function `Ψ`.

Hartle-Hawking (No-Boundary):
The prescription is a path integral over compact Euclidean geometries. In the semi-classical approximation, this leads to a wave function whose amplitude in the "Lorentzian" (classical) region is:
|Ψ_HH|² ∝ exp(+2 / 3V(φ))
This probability is maximized when the potential `V(φ)` is large. This is the model's key prediction: it naturally favors the conditions required for inflation. The universe is most likely to be born at the top of an inflationary potential.

Vilenkin (Tunneling):
The prescription is that the wave function at the `a=0` boundary of superspace should consist of only "outgoing" modes, corresponding to an expanding universe. This is analogous to the boundary condition for alpha decay in nuclear physics, where a particle tunnels out of a potential well. The resulting wave function, in the same approximation, has an amplitude of:
|Ψ_V|² ∝ exp(-2 / 3V(φ))
The sign in the exponent is flipped! This means the probability is maximized when the potential `V(φ)` is *small*. This model predicts that the universe is most likely to emerge at a low-energy state, from which it would be difficult to start inflation. Proponents of this model must then invoke anthropic reasoning or other mechanisms to explain why we find ourselves in a universe that did inflate.

This simulation allows you to directly see this dramatic difference. When you select Hartle-Hawking, the bright regions of high probability will appear at `φ` values where the potential `V(φ)` is high. When you select Vilenkin, the bright regions will shift to where `V(φ)` is low. This visualizes one of the deepest and most important debates in theoretical cosmology.
